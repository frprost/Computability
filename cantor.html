Cantor’s diagonal argument

There are “more” real numbers than integers. The technique of the proof, diagonalization, is the main weapon of all of computability theory.

There are “more” decimal numbers than whole numbers: they cannot all be numbered. The method of the proof, the diagonal, is the main weapon of all of computability.

PrerequisitesInjections, surjections, bijections

The reals are not countable

Suppose we had numbered all the numbers between 0 and 1, written with their decimal digits. Let us build a new number: its first digit differs from that of the first number in the list, its second from that of the second, and so on. This new number differs from every number in the list: the list was not complete. So the real numbers cannot all be numbered. The same argument, applied to programs, shows that there are functions that no program computes.

Theorem
Uncountability of ℝ (Cantor, 1891)

There is no bijection between $\N$ and $\R$ (not even a surjection from $\N$ onto $\R$).

In plain words. We cannot make a numbered list of all the real numbers (the decimal numbers, with as many digits as we like). There are strictly more of them than whole numbers.

Infinity does not come in a single size. We can number the integers, the fractions, the words of a dictionary, the programs: these sets are said to be countable. The real numbers are not: every list misses some.

The proof fits in one idea, the diagonal. Suppose we have a list of numbers between 0 and 1. We build a new number by changing the first digit of the first one, the second digit of the second, the third of the third, and so on. This number is equal to none of those in the list, since it differs from each one in at least one digit. So the list was incomplete.

This little device, which looks like a game, is the engine of almost all the impossibilities of this manual: the existence of functions that no program computes, the halting problem, Gödel’s incompleteness theorems.

It suffices to show that no sequence $(x_i)_{i\ge0}$ of reals in $]0,1[$ contains all of them, since $]0,1[$ is in bijection with $\R$: $t\mapsto\tan\big(\pi(t-\tfrac12)\big)$ is a bijection from $]0,1[$ onto $\R$ (the composite of $t\mapsto\pi(t-\frac12)$, a bijection from $]0,1[$ onto $]-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2[$, and of the tangent, a bijection from $]-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2[$ onto $\R$ whose inverse is the arctangent).

Write each $x_i=0.x_{i,1}x_{i,2}x_{i,3}\dots$ in decimal, choosing the proper expansion (the one that does not end with infinitely many 9s). Define $y=0.y_1y_2y_3\dots$ by

$$y_j=\begin{cases}5&\text{if }x_{j-1,j}\neq5,\\ 4&\text{if }x_{j-1,j}=5.\end{cases}$$

The number $y$ lies in $]0,1[$ and its expansion, made of 4s and 5s, is the only expansion of $y$ (it contains neither 0 nor 9). For every $i$, $y$ differs from $x_i$ in the $(i+1)$th decimal digit; since the expansions are unique, $y\ne x_i$. Hence $y$ is not in the sequence.

Why 4s and 5s? The most commonly quoted version takes $y_j=9-x_{j-1,j}$. It requires some care: a real number may have two expansions ($0.1000\ldots=0.0999\ldots$), and the $y$ so constructed could be, under its other expansion, one of the $x_i$. For example, if $x_0=0.2\ldots$, $x_1=0.8000\ldots$, and all diagonal digits from the second one on are $0$, one gets $y=0.7999\ldots=0.8=x_1$: the constructed number does appear in the list, under its proper expansion, although its digits differ from those of every $x_i$. The argument can be repaired by treating this case separately, but the simplest fix is to choose digits other than $0$ and $9$: an expansion with neither $0$ nor $9$ is the only way of writing the number.

The same diagonal, on binary sequences: if $h_0,h_1,h_2,\dots$ are functions from $\N$ to $\{0,1\}$, the function $h(n)=1-h_n(n)$ is none of the $h_n$.

0123…
$h_0$0110
$h_1$1100
$h_2$0001
$h_3$1011
$h$1010…

When the $h_n$ are the functions computed by the successive programs, $h$ is computed by no program: this is the proof that non-computable functions exist. When the diagonal is itself computable, as in the halting problem, one gets a contradiction, hence an impossibility.

Is a theory of everything possible?

One sometimes meets a philosophical argument, which we reproduce and discuss, because it illustrates well the reach and the limits of diagonalization outside mathematics.

Argument
There are more phenomena than explanations

Every explanation can be written down, so explanations form a countable set. If the phenomena to be explained form an uncountable set, some phenomena have no explanation.

In plain words. An explanation is written with an alphabet, as a finite text. So all possible explanations can be numbered. If the phenomena to be explained are more numerous than the integers, some will never have an explanation.

The argument looks good, but it must be discussed. Two objections weaken it.

  • A question that cannot even be formulated is not really a question. Now the questions that can be asked are also written as finite texts: so there are countably many of them, like explanations.
  • A scientific law does not explain facts one by one. The law of falling bodies fits in one line and holds for all possible heights, of which there are infinitely many, even uncountably many.

A sturdier version of the idea goes through computation rather than through numbers. If a theory is a system from which predictions are drawn mechanically, there are questions that can be written in its language and that it will never be able to answer: this is the incompleteness theorem. It is not just a logician’s curiosity: in 2015, three physicists (Cubitt, Pérez-García and Wolf) showed that a precise question about certain quantum materials has no computable answer.
