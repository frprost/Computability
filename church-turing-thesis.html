The Church-Turing thesis

A claim that is not a theorem and cannot be one: the functions computable by an effective procedure are exactly the functions computable by a Turing machine.

Everything that can be computed by following a mechanical method can be computed by a Turing machine. This claim, the Church-Turing thesis, is not a theorem and cannot be one; yet it is accepted by almost everyone.

The previous chapters showed that three very different definitions of computation give exactly the same functions. But how can we know that these definitions capture all possible methods of computation, including those nobody has yet imagined? It cannot be proved, because “method of computation” is not a precise mathematical notion. It can only be asserted, with good reasons for believing it. This is the Church-Turing thesis, one of the rare fundamental statements of science that belongs at once to mathematics, physics and philosophy.

Statement of the thesis

The thesis says: everything a person can compute with paper, a pencil and a precise method, a Turing machine can compute. It links an intuitive idea (“mechanical method”) with a precise definition (“Turing machine”), as physics linked the intuitive idea of heat with the temperature we measure.

Thesis
Church-Turing thesis

A function from $\N^n$ to $\N$ is computable by an effective procedure (a mechanical, unambiguous method, which can be carried out with paper and pencil in finitely many steps, without intuition or ingenuity) if and only if it is Turing-computable, that is, recursive, that is, λ-definable.

In plain words. The Church-Turing thesis asserts this: everything that can be computed by following a mechanical method, with pencil and paper, without ever needing any trick or intuition, a Turing machine can compute. And conversely.

It is not a theorem, and this is not a flaw. “Mechanical method” is an everyday idea, which has no mathematical definition. The thesis proposes to replace it with a precise notion, as physics replaced the vague idea of “heat” with a quantity that is measured, temperature. Such a proposal can be supported, criticized, perhaps one day refuted by a counterexample. It cannot be proved.

Yet it is accepted by almost everyone, for three reasons.

  • Everyone finds the same thing. Researchers who started from very different ideas (Turing machines, recursive functions, λ-calculus, modern programming languages) arrived at exactly the same list of computable functions.
  • Turing watched a human compute. He noticed that a human computer looks at only a small part of the sheet at a time, has only a limited number of “states of mind”, and performs only simple actions. He showed that this behaviour reduces to his machine.
  • No counterexample. Since 1936, nobody has found a mechanical method that a Turing machine cannot imitate.

Gödel, sceptical at first, was convinced by Turing’s analysis; he wrote that one finally had a “precise and unquestionably adequate” definition of what a mechanical procedure is.

One direction is a theorem in the sense that nobody disputes it: every Turing-computable function is effectively computable (a Turing machine is an effective procedure). The other direction is the thesis proper.

The mathematician Robin Gandy (1980) proposed an analysis of machines (no longer of humans) that compute discretely, deterministically, with a bounded speed of propagation and parts of bounded size, and proved that everything such a machine computes is Turing-computable (Gandy’s theorem, below). Under these precise hypotheses, the thesis becomes a theorem; the question then shifts to the relevance of the hypotheses.

Theorem
Discrete machines with local causality are Turing-computable (Gandy, 1980, in Sieg’s form)

A machine with local causality is given by a finite alphabet $\Lambda$, a maximal degree $\Delta$ and two finite tables $R$ and $E$ described below. A state is a finite undirected graph whose vertices carry labels from $\Lambda$ and have degree at most $\Delta$, considered up to isomorphism. The type $\tau(v)$ of a vertex $v$ is the isomorphism class of its radius-$1$ neighbourhood (the vertex, its neighbours, their labels and the edges between them, $v$ being distinguished); there are only finitely many possible types. The table $R$ associates with each type $\tau$ a finite labelled graph $R(\tau)$ whose vertices are numbered $1,\dots,m_\tau$; the table $E$ associates with each pair of types $(\tau,\tau')$ a set $E(\tau,\tau')$ of pairs $(i,j)$ with $i\le m_\tau$, $j\le m_{\tau'}$. The successor $F(X)$ of a state $X$ is obtained by replacing, simultaneously, each vertex $v$ by a copy $R_v$ of $R(\tau(v))$, and by joining, for each edge $vw$ of $X$, vertex $i$ of $R_v$ to vertex $j$ of $R_w$ for $(i,j)\in E(\tau(v),\tau(w))$ (the tables are assumed such that the result still has degree at most $\Delta$, and $E(\tau',\tau)$ symmetric to $E(\tau,\tau')$). Some labels are declared terminal: the computation stops when a vertex carries such a label.

Theorem. The function $F$, read on codes of states, is recursive; consequently, every function computed by a machine with local causality (inputs and outputs encoded by states) is Turing-computable.

In plain words. Take any machine made of finitely many parts, each of limited size, in which each part is influenced only by its neighbours. Even if all the parts work at the same time, this machine computes nothing more than a Turing machine.

Turing was thinking of a human computing, step by step, on a corner of the sheet. A machine can do more: act everywhere at once, like the thousands of processors of a graphics card, and even transform itself along the way. Gandy looked for the weakest physical conditions that guarantee that such a machine stays within Turing’s limits. He found four, which seem natural: the machine is made of finitely many pieces; its working does not depend on the names given to these pieces; the pieces cannot be infinitely small; and information propagates step by step, at limited speed.

Under these conditions, the thesis becomes a real theorem. The discussion shifts: do these conditions describe the real world well? The imaginary machines that would “compute more” than Turing’s, what is called hypercomputation, always break one of them, for example with instantaneous transmission or infinitely small pieces.

A state is encoded by an integer: number its $N$ vertices $1,\dots,N$, and encode the list of labels and the list of edges. The same state has several codes (one per numbering); two codes represent the same state if and only if some permutation of $\{1,\dots,N\}$ maps one to the other, which is checked by trying the $N!$ permutations. It therefore suffices to compute, from a code of $X$, one code of $F(X)$.

  • Types. For each vertex $v$, extract the radius-$1$ neighbourhood (at most $\Delta+1$ vertices) and look, among the representatives of the types (finitely many, fixed once and for all together with the tables), for the one to which it is isomorphic by an isomorphism fixing $v$: a finite search among at most $(\Delta+1)!$ bijections.
  • Replacement. Number the vertices of $F(X)$ by the pairs $(v,i)$, $i\le m_{\tau(v)}$, in lexicographic order; copy the labels and internal edges of $R(\tau(v))$, then, for each edge $vw$ of $X$ and each $(i,j)\in E(\tau(v),\tau(w))$, the edge $(v,i)(w,j)$.

Each step is a bounded loop over finite objects, from the finite tables $R$ and $E$: $F$ is primitive recursive on codes, and the result does not depend, up to isomorphism, on the numbering chosen for $X$, since the construction uses only the types and the edges. The predicate “the state with code $c$ has a terminal vertex” is primitive recursive. The computation of the machine on the initial state $X_0$ is the sequence $X_{n+1}=F(X_n)$; the “result” function is computed by minimization: search for the first $n$ such that $X_n$ is terminal, and decode the output. This is a partial recursive function, hence Turing-computable (equivalence of the models).

Cellular automata (fixed graph, $R(\tau)$ reduced to one vertex), Turing machines (the tape is a path, the head a label) and branching networks are machines with local causality. Gandy (“Church’s thesis and principles for mechanisms”, 1980) starts from more abstract hypotheses: states are hereditarily finite sets built on atoms, the dynamics commutes with permutations of the atoms, and the “principle of local causality” says that the next state is assembled from regions determined by parts of bounded size. The longest part of his paper derives from these principles a finite local description of the kind given by our tables $R$ and $E$. Sieg (2002) showed that this local description can be taken directly as an axiom, the proof of computability then becoming the one just given: this is the point of view adopted here.

Remark
Three further justifications: sociological, linguistic, historical

Three further arguments are often put forward in support of the thesis: a sociological argument (for nearly a century, every new model of computation proposed has computed exactly the Turing-computable functions, or fewer if it is too weak); a linguistic argument (every machine must be describable by a blueprint, otherwise a “magic” part would remain unexplained, and a blueprint is a text); a historical argument (very old models, such as Diophantine equations, define exactly the same sets as Turing machines).

In plain words. Three other arguments support the thesis. All the models of computation proposed for nearly a century compute the same thing. All scientific knowledge can be written as text, hence processed by a machine. And mathematicians who were not thinking about computation at all ran into the same border.

The second argument deserves attention. Books, articles, photos, videos, measurements: all scientific output can be digitized, that is, written as a long sequence of 0s and 1s. A mere text editor could, in principle, produce all of it. All of science thus fits into a small piece of itself, computer science. This is the same idea as Gödel’s, who turns formulas and proofs into whole numbers (Incompleteness). Consequence: the limits of computation apply to every precise discourse, hence to all sciences, not only to computer science.

The third argument is historical. A problem posed by Diophantus nearly two thousand years ago, solving equations in whole numbers, turned out to follow exactly the same border as Turing machines (see Diophantine equations).

A set $A\subseteq\N$ is Diophantine if there is a polynomial $P(a,x_1,\dots,x_k)$ with integer coefficients such that $a\in A\iff\exists x_1,\dots,x_k\in\N\ P(a,x_1,\dots,x_k)=0$. Matiyasevich’s theorem (1970), the culmination of the work of Davis, Putnam and J. Robinson (MRDP theorem), says: the Diophantine sets are exactly the semi-decidable sets. For example $\{a\mid\exists x,y\ 47x^2+12y^3-a=0\}$ is semi-decidable: enumerate the pairs $(x,y)$ until a solution is found. This yields a negative answer to Hilbert’s tenth problem: there is no algorithm deciding whether a Diophantine equation has a solution, since there are semi-decidable sets that are not decidable. The chapter Diophantine equations proves this theorem and explains why the coincidence of the two classes is an argument for the thesis (universal equation).

The sociological argument is the “convergence” seen above. The linguistic argument rests on the finiteness of descriptions: a program is a finite word over a finite alphabet, which suffices to show that there are only countably many computable functions.

Reasoning “by Church’s thesis”

In practice, nobody writes Turing machines. To show that something can be computed, one describes the method in plain language, step by step, and relies on the thesis to assert that a machine exists.

The practice, very widespread in computability texts, of merely describing an algorithm informally in order to conclude that a function is computable, is sometimes called “arguing by Church’s thesis”.

Principle
Proofs by Church’s thesis

To show that a function is computable, it suffices to describe an algorithm computing it, in ordinary language, provided each step can manifestly be mechanized. The thesis guarantees that one could, if one wished, write the corresponding Turing machine.

In plain words. To show that something is computable, it is enough to describe a precise method in ordinary language. The thesis guarantees that it could be translated into a Turing machine if one wished.

This is what all computer scientists do: they describe an algorithm in sentences or in “pseudo-code”, and nobody writes the corresponding Turing machine. This habit is sound because the path from a description to the machine is long but holds no mystery. The compiler supplied with this manual travels this path in full, for a family of programs.

This is not an illegitimate logical shortcut: each appeal to the thesis can be replaced by an explicit construction, and reference works (Rogers, 1967) do so systematically in principle. The risk is real only for arguments in which the “algorithm” uses non-computable information (for example “if the program halts, then…”): the thesis does not exempt one from checking that each test is effective.

In this manual, the technological level replaces appeals to the thesis by actual constructions as far as possible: executable machines, reduced terms, compilation.

Variants and extensions

One may ask whether nature itself, and not only a person computing, obeys the thesis: can a physical phenomenon, a quantum computer, a brain compute what no Turing machine computes? These questions are open, and they touch on what we know of the world.

Thesis
Physical Church-Turing thesis and extended thesis

Physical thesis: every function computable by a physical system (not only by a human following rules) is Turing-computable. Extended (or “strong”) thesis: every physically realizable model of computation can be simulated by a Turing machine with at most a polynomial overhead in time.

In plain words. The thesis has two variants that speak about the physical world. The physical version says: everything a real device can compute, a Turing machine can compute too. The extended version goes further: everything a real device computes quickly, a Turing machine also computes reasonably quickly. The first withstands everything we know. The second is threatened by quantum computers.

These two claims are no longer questions of logic but hypotheses about nature: they can be discussed as a physical law is discussed.

  • The quantum computer. It computes nothing more than an ordinary computer, because it can be imitated on an ordinary computer, very slowly. So it does not threaten the physical version. But it seems to go much faster for certain problems: Shor’s algorithm (1994) breaks a large number into prime factors very quickly, whereas no ordinary method that fast is known. See Randomness, approximation and quantum computing.
  • Hypercomputation. Devices have been imagined that would perform infinitely many steps in finite time, for example by going twice as fast at each step. All of them require infinite precision, energy or stability. None can be built with known physics.
  • Deutsch’s formulation. The physicist David Deutsch (1985) asserts that every “realizable” finite physical system can be perfectly imitated by a universal machine. The word “realizable” hides a difficulty: it is easy to confuse what we know of the world (our theories) with what the world is. A theory is asked to be accurate where it is tested, not to describe everything. This point is developed just below (physics is a text, the living is not a blueprint).
  • The human mind. If the physical version is true and if the brain is a physical system like any other, then everything a human can compute, a machine can. The philosopher Lucas (1961) and the physicist Penrose (1989) argued, from Gödel’s theorems, that the mind surpasses machines; most logicians judge their reasoning to be flawed (see Incompleteness).

The extended thesis is the foundation of complexity theory: it gives a model-independent meaning to the class P of “feasible” problems. Its probabilistic version (BPP instead of P) is challenged by quantum computing (class BQP), if one accepts the unproved conjecture that factoring is not in BPP.

Question
Physics is a text, the living is not a blueprint

What is called “physics” is not reality but a model of reality. A model must be explicitly writable; physical theories are therefore finite texts, and they form a countable set. They are thereby subject to diagonal arguments: no enumerable family of predictive theories predicts all possible behaviours, nor even all computable behaviours.

Deutsch’s thesis is true, almost by construction, of the systems we build by following a blueprint written in the language of a theory. The living did not come from a blueprint. There may be a countable way of formalizing it, but we have no proof of that; and if there is none, we will never be able to know.

In plain words. Physics is a description of the world, written in a language. So it is not surprising that it obeys the limits of computation, which are the limits of every precise language. Nothing guarantees that the world itself, and in particular the living, obeys the same limits.

Two senses of the word “proof”. In mathematics, a proof is a chain of rules that can be checked line by line, without even understanding what it says. In physics, a “proof” is an agreement between a theory and experiments: the theory holds as long as counterexamples are sought without being found. Newton’s mechanics is “proved” within its domain, which relativity later enlarged. Saying that Deutsch “proved” that every physical system can be imitated by a computer, and concluding that the brain is a computer, is mixing the two senses of the word.

An experiment is a text. Setting up an experiment means first writing the protocol that describes it. Testing a theory means computing its prediction and comparing it with a measurement, which has only finitely many digits. So we can only speak of the experiments we know how to describe, and these descriptions obey the limits of computation. It is not surprising that all the experiments built confirm Deutsch’s principle: it describes the limits of the language in which they are conceived. Requiring reality to bend to it is laying it on the bed of Procrustes, the brigand of Greek mythology who stretched or cut his guests so that they had exactly the length of his bed.

The diagonal, again. Because theories are texts, they can be numbered, and everything that can be numbered lends itself to Cantor’s argument. Faced with any list of theories that predict the future, one can imagine a behaviour that contradicts the first theory at the first observation, the second at the second, and so on: none predicts it. This is not merely abstract. A system that reads the prediction made about it and does the opposite necessarily defeats the predictor (see the theorem on predictors). Every written theory has blind spots of this kind.

The living. A brain, a cell, an organism were not made from a blueprint: they are the result of a long history, evolution. Nothing guarantees in advance that they are “realizable systems” in Deutsch’s sense. To apply his thesis to the brain, this point would first have to be established, and that is precisely what is missing. One may hope to get there: propose a description, program it, and check that the program behaves like the living system. But if no description of this kind exists, no experiment will show it; we will only see, endlessly, attempts failing. A success can be observed; an impossibility of this type cannot.

This text does not say that the living escapes computation. It says that the question is open, and that one of the two answers is beyond the reach of experiment. It takes up a post by the author of this manual, “Mathematical Necessity, Nature and the bed of Procrustes” (2023).

The two underlying mathematical arguments are elementary; it is their physical reading that is debatable.

Cardinality. Fix a finite alphabet. Theories, being finite texts, form a countable set $\{T_0,T_1,\dots\}$ (enumerate the words by length and then alphabetically). Model the observable behaviour of a system by an infinite sequence of binary measurement results, $s\in\{0,1\}^{\N}$. Say that $T_i$ predicts $s$ if $T_i$ determines each of the $s_n$. Each theory predicts at most one sequence: $i\mapsto$ “the sequence predicted by $T_i$” is a partial function from $\N$ onto the set of predicted sequences, which is therefore countable. But $\{0,1\}^\N$ is not (Cantor). Better, for the uniform measure (independent bits, of probability $1/2$), each sequence has measure zero, hence so does the countable set of predicted sequences: a “random” sequence is predicted by no theory, with probability 1.

Effective diagonal. Let $(P_i)$ be an enumerable list of total computable predictors, where $P_i(n)\in\{0,1\}$ predicts the $n$-th observation. The sequence $d_n=1-P_n(n)$ is computable (compute $P_n$, then evaluate it at $n$), and it differs from the prediction of $P_i$ at rank $i$, for every $i$. Thus no enumerable list of total predictors covers even the computable behaviours: each time, one more theory is needed. This is why the total computable functions cannot be enumerated (this is the diagonal argument given for $\mathrm{Tot}$ in the proof of the reductions of K). If partial predictors are accepted, the list becomes enumerable (all machines), but one can no longer decide which ones answer: the diagonal turns into the halting problem.

What these arguments do not show. They do not show that the real world produces non-computable behaviours: it might produce only computable ones, or be observable only to finite precision, in which case every finite sequence of measurements is trivially predicted by some theory (the list of results). They show that the hypothesis “reality is described by a theory” is a restriction, and that every family of written theories lets possible behaviours escape. Whether the living falls into these blind spots is an empirical question, and we have seen that only a positive answer could be established.

Question
Is consciousness Turing-computable?

Nobody knows whether human consciousness (or, more modestly, the behaviour of a human being) can be reproduced by a Turing machine. The two possible answers do not have the same status: a positive answer might one day be established scientifically, a negative answer could not be by the usual means of science.

In plain words. Nobody knows whether a machine could reproduce human consciousness. Showing that it can would be possible in principle: it would be enough to build the machine. Showing that it cannot seems beyond the reach of science.

If consciousness is computable, we can proceed as usual in science: propose a theory of consciousness, program it, and check that the program behaves as predicted. It is difficult, but it is an ordinary approach.

If it is not, how could this be proved? A scientific theory must make predictions that can be checked; so it works, in a way, like a machine. But a machine that completely described a non-computable consciousness would be a contradiction. So we could never have a complete theory of such a consciousness; we would only observe, indefinitely, that the attempts fail. The philosopher Karl Popper said that a claim is scientific if an experiment can, in principle, contradict it. “Consciousness is not computable” does not pass this test.

We find again the asymmetry of the halting problem: that a program halts can be seen; that it never halts cannot.

The analogy can be made precise. A statement of the form “there is a program $e$ satisfying $P$”, where $P(e)$ is decidable, is semi-decidable: a witness $e$ suffices to confirm it. Its negation, “no program satisfies $P$”, has the form $\Pi_1$ (arithmetical hierarchy) and admits no finite certificate in general. The argument above assumes that “describing consciousness” is a property of this type, which is itself an idealization: we do not know how to formalize $P$. Note also that in mathematics negative statements are routinely proved (halting is not decidable); the asymmetry here concerns experimental verification, not proof.

What the thesis does not say

The thesis is often misquoted. It does not say that everything is computation, nor that the brain is a computer, nor that every problem has a computable solution. It says only where the border of what mechanical methods can reach lies.

  • It does not say that everything is computation: it delimits what is computable, and the rest of the manual shows that many precise questions are not.
  • It says nothing about efficiency (that is the extended thesis, more fragile).
  • It concerns only functions on finite objects (integers, words). For real numbers or continuous functions, there is a theory of computability (computable analysis) with its own choices of definitions.
