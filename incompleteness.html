Computation and logic: incompleteness and the decision problem

The undecidability of halting yields in a few lines Gödel’s theorems and the negative answer to Hilbert’s decision problem.

In 1931, Kurt Gödel showed that no system of axioms can prove all truths about the whole numbers. With the impossibility of predicting whether programs halt, this theorem becomes almost obvious.

PrerequisitesSemi-decidability · Predicate logic

At the beginning of the twentieth century, the great mathematician David Hilbert dreamed of putting mathematics beyond all doubt: writing once and for all a list of axioms, starting truths, from which all mathematical truths could be deduced mechanically, and proving that this list never leads to a contradiction. He even hoped for a mechanical method able to say, for every statement, whether it is provable.

Kurt Gödel in 1931, then Alonzo Church and Alan Turing in 1936, showed that this dream cannot be fulfilled. This chapter explains why, building on what we already know: if everything about numbers could be proved, we could predict whether programs halt, which is impossible.

The language of arithmetic

To speak about whole numbers, we use a very poor language: variables, zero, “plus one”, addition, multiplication, equality, “and”, “or”, “not”, “there exists”, “for all”. The first discovery is that this primary-school language is enough to describe any computation: “program number $e$ halts” translates into a sentence about numbers.

We consider the first-order formulas built with variables, the constants $0,1$, the operations $+$ and $\times$, equality, the connectives $\neg,\wedge,\vee,\Rightarrow$ and the quantifiers $\forall,\exists$ ranging over the integers (see the appendix Predicate logic). A sentence is a formula with no free variable; it is true if it is true in $\N$ with the usual addition and multiplication. We write $\underline n$ for the term $(\cdots((0+1)+1)\cdots)+1$ ($n$ times $+1$), which denotes the integer $n$.

Definition
Bounded formulas and Σ₁ formulas

The bounded (or $\Delta_0$) formulas are built from equalities $t_1=t_2$ by the connectives and the bounded quantifiers $\exists x<t$ and $\forall x<t$ ($x$ not occurring in the term $t$). A $\Sigma_1$ formula has the form $\exists y\,\theta$ with $\theta$ bounded. A relation $R\subseteq\N^k$ is $\Sigma_1$ if there is a $\Sigma_1$ formula $\phi(x_1,\dots,x_k)$ such that $R(\vec n)$ holds if and only if $\phi(\underline{n_1},\dots,\underline{n_k})$ is true. A partial function is $\Sigma_1$ if its graph $\{(\vec x,y):f(\vec x)=y\}$ is. We write $x\le t$ for $x<t+1$.

The $\Sigma_1$ relations are exactly the semi-decidable sets, that is, the class $\Sigma_1$ of the arithmetical hierarchy. A bounded formula is decided by examining all cases, so a $\Sigma_1$ relation is semi-decidable. The converse is the representability proposition below.

In plain words. A sentence about numbers that speaks only of a limited number of cases (“all the numbers below 100 are…”) can be checked by a computation. A sentence of the form “there is a number such that…”, followed by such a check, can be confirmed by finding that number.

The former are the logical equivalent of “repeat $n$ times” loops, the latter of searches that may never end.

Lemma
Closure of Σ₁ relations

The $\Sigma_1$ relations contain the relations defined by bounded formulas, and are closed under $\wedge$, $\vee$, existential quantification $\exists x$, bounded universal quantification $\forall x<t$, and substitution of terms for variables.

Let $\exists y\,\theta$ and $\exists y'\,\theta'$ be two $\Sigma_1$ formulas ($y,y'$ renamed to be distinct and new). We have:

  • $\exists y\,\theta\wedge\exists y'\,\theta'$ is equivalent to $\exists w\,\exists y\le w\,\exists y'\le w\,(\theta\wedge\theta')$, and likewise for $\vee$;
  • $\exists x\,\exists y\,\theta$ is equivalent to $\exists w\,\exists x\le w\,\exists y\le w\,\theta$;
  • $\forall x<t\ \exists y\,\theta(x,y)$ is equivalent to $\exists w\,\forall x<t\ \exists y<w\ \theta(x,y)$. The forward direction is the only non-obvious one: for each value $x$ less than the value of $t$ (there are finitely many), choose a witness $y_x$, and take for $w$ an integer larger than all the $y_x$.

A bounded formula $\theta$ is equivalent to $\exists y\,(y=y\wedge\theta)$. Substituting a term for a free variable preserves boundedness (renaming bound variables).

Lemma
Gödel’s β function

Let $\beta(c,d,i)$ be the remainder of the division of $c$ by $1+(i+1)d$.

  1. The relation $\beta(c,d,i)=r$ is defined by a bounded formula.
  2. For every finite sequence $a_0,\dots,a_n$ of integers, there are $c,d$ such that $\beta(c,d,i)=a_i$ for every $i\le n$.

In plain words. With only addition and multiplication, a finite list of numbers, of any length, can be coded by two numbers.

This is an ingenious arithmetical trick of Gödel’s, which uses remainders of divisions. It makes it possible to speak of sequences of computation, hence of programs, in the language of numbers.

(1) $\beta(c,d,i)=r\iff r<1+(i+1)\times d\ \wedge\ \exists q\le c\ \big(c=q\times(1+(i+1)\times d)+r\big)$.

(2) Let $s=\max(n,a_0,\dots,a_n)+1$, $d=s!$ and $m_i=1+(i+1)d$ for $i\le n$.

  • The $m_i$ are pairwise coprime. If a prime $p$ divides $m_i$ and $m_j$ with $i<j$, it divides $m_j-m_i=(j-i)d$. It does not divide $d$, otherwise it would divide $m_i-(i+1)d=1$. By Euclid’s lemma, $p$ divides $j-i\le n<s$, so $p\le s$ and $p$ divides $s!=d$: contradiction.
  • Chinese remainders. Let $M=m_0m_1\cdots m_n$. Consider the map $c\mapsto(c\bmod m_0,\dots,c\bmod m_n)$ from $\{0,\dots,M-1\}$ to $\prod_i\{0,\dots,m_i-1\}$. It is injective: if $c$ and $c'$ have the same remainders, each $m_i$ divides $|c-c'|$. Now if pairwise coprime integers divide $N$, their product divides $N$ (by induction on their number: if $m_0\cdots m_{k-1}$ divides $N$, write $N=m_0\cdots m_{k-1}N'$; a prime factor $p$ of $m_k$ divides no $m_i$, $i<k$, so, by Euclid’s lemma, it divides $N'$; repeating with $m_k/p$ and $N'/p$, one gets that $m_k$ divides $N'$). So $M$ divides $|c-c'|<M$, and $c=c'$. Both sets having $M$ elements, the map is also surjective.
  • Since $a_i<s\le d<m_i$, there is therefore $c<M$ with $c\bmod m_i=a_i$, that is, $\beta(c,d,i)=a_i$, for every $i\le n$.

For the sequence $3,1,4$: $s=5$, $d=120$, the moduli are $121$, $241$, $361$, and $c=5{,}744{,}236$ works ($c\bmod121=3$, $c\bmod241=1$, $c\bmod361=4$). The numbers quickly become enormous; only their existence matters.

Proposition
Recursive functions and semi-decidable sets are definable in arithmetic

Every (partial) recursive function is $\Sigma_1$. Consequently, for every semi-decidable set $A$, there is a formula $\exists y\,\theta(x,y)$, with $\theta$ bounded, such that $n\in A$ if and only if $\exists y\,\theta(\underline n,y)$ is true. In particular, there is a $\Sigma_1$ formula $\kappa(x)$ such that $e\in K\iff\kappa(\underline e)$ is true, and the relation $z=x^y$ is $\Sigma_1$: exponentiation is definable with $+$ and $\times$.

In plain words. Whatever a program computes can be said by a sentence about whole numbers written with addition and multiplication.

School arithmetic is enough to speak of all computations. Consequently, every difficulty about programs becomes a difficulty about numbers.

By induction on the definition of $f$ (recursive functions), using the closure lemma at each step.

  • Base functions: $y=\underline m$, $y=x+1$, $y=x_i$ are bounded formulas.
  • Substitution $f=\sigma[g,h_1,\dots,h_k]$: $y=f(\vec x)\iff\exists z_1\cdots\exists z_k\,\big(z_1=h_1(\vec x)\wedge\cdots\wedge z_k=h_k(\vec x)\wedge y=g(\vec z)\big)$, which indeed requires each $h_i(\vec x)$ to be defined.
  • Recursion $f=\rho[g,h]$: $y=f(n,\vec x)$ if and only if $$\exists c\,\exists d\,\Big[\exists u\,\big(u=g(\vec x)\wedge\beta(c,d,0)=u\big)\ \wedge\ \forall i<n\ \exists v\,\exists w\,\big(\beta(c,d,i)=v\wedge\beta(c,d,i+1)=w\wedge w=h(i,v,\vec x)\big)\ \wedge\ \beta(c,d,n)=y\Big].$$ If this formula is true, the sequence $a_i=\beta(c,d,i)$ satisfies $a_0=g(\vec x)$ and $a_{i+1}=h(i,a_i,\vec x)$, so $a_i=f(i,\vec x)$ by induction on $i\le n$, and $y=a_n$. Conversely, if $f(n,\vec x)=y$, the values $f(0,\vec x),\dots,f(n,\vec x)$ are all defined, and the β lemma provides $c,d$ coding them.
  • Minimization $f=\mu[g]$: $y=f(\vec x)\iff g(\vec x,y)=0\ \wedge\ \forall z<y\ \exists v\,\big(v=g(\vec x,z)\wedge\neg(v=0)\big)$, which translates the definition exactly (the values $g(\vec x,z)$, $z<y$, must be defined and nonzero).

A semi-decidable set is the domain of a computable function $\varphi$, which is recursive (equivalence of models): $n\in A\iff\exists y\,(y=\varphi(n))$, a $\Sigma_1$ relation by closure. For $K$, take $\varphi(e)=u(e,e)$. Finally $(x,y)\mapsto x^y$ is primitive recursive.

Kleene’s predicate $T_1$ is a special case: one could have translated directly “$y$ codes a computation of machine $e$ on $e$”, but the proof by induction avoids writing this formula. A stronger form (MRDP theorem) shows that bounded quantifiers can even be dispensed with.

The incompleteness theorem

Suppose that a system of axioms proves all true sentences about numbers, and nothing false. To know whether a program halts, it would then be enough to search, among all possible proofs, for a proof of “it halts” or a proof of “it does not halt”; one of the two would eventually turn up. We could therefore solve the halting problem. This is impossible: such a system does not exist.

Theorem
First incompleteness theorem (computational form)

Let $\mathsf T$ be a theory of arithmetic whose axioms form a decidable set (one can check mechanically that a text is a proof) and which is sound (it proves only true sentences). Then there is a true sentence that $\mathsf T$ does not prove. It can even be chosen of the form “program $e$ does not halt on $e$”.

In plain words. For every system of axioms about numbers whose proofs can be checked mechanically, and which proves nothing false, there are true sentences that it does not prove.

No system of axioms, however rich, captures all truths about the whole numbers. The missing sentence can be added as a new axiom; the new system will in turn have truths that it does not prove. This is not a flaw of this or that system: it is a limit of every purely mechanical approach. Mathematical truth always goes beyond what a fixed set of rules can reach of it.

Since the axioms of $\mathsf T$ are decidable, the set of theorems of $\mathsf T$ is semi-decidable (enumerate all proofs). Consider $B=\{e: \mathsf T\vdash\neg\kappa(\underline e)\}$, which is semi-decidable. By soundness of $\mathsf T$, $B\subseteq\overline K$. Since $\overline K$ is not semi-decidable (corollary), $B\neq\overline K$: there is $e\in\overline K\setminus B$, that is, such that $\neg\kappa(\underline e)$ is true ($e$ does not halt on $e$) but not provable in $\mathsf T$.

One can even build $e$ explicitly: by the recursion theorem, let $e$ be the program which, on any input, searches for a proof of $\neg\kappa(\underline e)$ in $\mathsf T$ and halts if it finds one. If $\mathsf T$ proved $\neg\kappa(\underline e)$, then $e$ would halt, and $\mathsf T$ would prove a false sentence. So $\mathsf T$ does not prove it, $e$ does not halt, and $\neg\kappa(\underline e)$ is true.

Gödel (1931) obtains the same result under a weaker hypothesis than soundness (ω-consistency), and Rosser (1936) under consistency alone, with sentences built by the diagonal lemma, the logical analogue of the recursion theorem: see Rosser’s theorem below.

The program of the proof, in pseudo-code (the function is_a_proof is a mechanical checker, which exists because the axioms are decidable):

gödel(x):
    me := text of this program                            // recursion theorem
    for n = 0, 1, 2, ...:
        if is_a_proof(n, "program me does not halt on me"):
            halt

This program does not halt, but the theory cannot prove it. Statements of this kind have been realized concretely: Yedidia and Aaronson (2016) built a Turing machine with 7,918 states whose non-termination is not provable in the set theory ZFC (assuming that an extension of ZFC by a large-cardinal axiom is consistent); later work produced machines with fewer than 800 states that halt if and only if the theory ZF is inconsistent. See also the corresponding theorem for busy beavers.

Peano arithmetic and the second theorem

Gödel proved more: a reasonable system of axioms cannot prove its own consistency, that is, the fact that it never leads to a contradiction. This section gives the complete proof for Peano arithmetic, the usual system of axioms for whole numbers.

The second theorem concerns what a theory proves about itself. One must therefore fix a proof system and a precise theory, and check that the theory can reason about its own proofs. This is the purpose of the lemmas that follow.

Definition
Proofs, Peano arithmetic, provability predicate

Proofs. We fix the following Hilbert calculus. Logical axioms: the instances of propositional tautologies; $\forall x\,\phi\Rightarrow\phi[x:=t]$ ($t$ substitutable for $x$); $\forall x\,(\phi\Rightarrow\psi)\Rightarrow(\phi\Rightarrow\forall x\,\psi)$ ($x$ not free in $\phi$); $x=x$ and $x=y\Rightarrow(\phi[z:=x]\Rightarrow\phi[z:=y])$ for atomic $\phi$. Rules: modus ponens (from $\phi$ and $\phi\Rightarrow\psi$, infer $\psi$) and generalization (from $\phi$, infer $\forall x\,\phi$). We write $\exists x\,\phi$ for $\neg\forall x\,\neg\phi$. A proof in a theory $\mathsf T$ (a set of sentences, its axioms) is a finite sequence of formulas each of which is a logical axiom, an axiom of $\mathsf T$, or follows from earlier ones by a rule; $\mathsf T\vdash\phi$ if $\phi$ is the last formula of a proof.

Peano arithmetic $\mathsf{PA}$: the axioms $\neg(x+1=0)$; $x+1=y+1\Rightarrow x=y$; $x+0=x$; $x+(y+1)=(x+y)+1$; $x\times0=0$; $x\times(y+1)=(x\times y)+x$ (universal closures), and the induction schema $\phi(0)\wedge\forall x\,(\phi(x)\Rightarrow\phi(x+1))\Rightarrow\forall x\,\phi(x)$ for every formula $\phi$. We write $x<t$ for $\exists z\,(x+(z+1)=t)$, and treat $\exists x<t$, $\forall x<t$ as bounded quantifiers.

Coding. Number the symbols, and code formulas (words) and proofs (sequences of formulas) by integers, as for words and sequences of words; $\ulcorner\phi\urcorner$ is the code of $\phi$. We consider theories $\mathsf T$ containing $\mathsf{PA}$ whose set of axiom codes is primitive recursive. Then the relation “$p$ codes a proof in $\mathsf T$ of the formula with code $f$” is primitive recursive (check each formula of the sequence). If $\chi$ is its characteristic function, set $\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(p,f)=\phi_\chi(p,f,\underline1)$, where $\phi_\chi$ is the formula given by the previous proposition, then

$$\mathrm{Prov}_{\mathsf T}(f)=\exists p\ \mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(p,f),\qquad \Box\phi=\mathrm{Prov}_{\mathsf T}(\underline{\ulcorner\phi\urcorner}),\qquad \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf T)=\neg\,\Box(0=1).$$

The axiomhood of $\mathsf T$ is defined by a disjunction “axiom of $\mathsf{PA}$ or other axiom”, so that $\mathsf{PA}$ trivially proves that every axiom of $\mathsf{PA}$ is an axiom of $\mathsf T$.

Σ formulas. In what follows, a Σ formula is built from the formulas $t_1=t_2$ and $\neg(t_1=t_2)$ by $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\exists x$, $\exists x<t$ and $\forall x<t$. All the formulas written in the definability proposition are of this form (before being put into the form $\exists y\,\theta$, which is no longer needed); in particular $\mathrm{Prov}_{\mathsf T}$ is a Σ formula.

In plain words. A proof is a text that follows precise rules; so it can be coded by a number, and “being a proof” becomes a property of numbers that a computation checks. Peano arithmetic is the usual system of axioms for the integers, with reasoning by induction.

A theory that speaks about numbers can thus speak about its own proofs, without knowing it.

Lemma
Σ-completeness

Every true Σ sentence is provable in $\mathsf{PA}$. Moreover, every bounded sentence is provable in $\mathsf{PA}$ if it is true, and refutable if it is false.

In plain words. Peano arithmetic proves all true sentences of the form “there is a number such that… (simple check)”.

To prove that a computation halts, it is enough to run it and write down all its steps. It is the “for all…” sentences that escape.

(a) Computations. By induction on $n$ (in the metalanguage), $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\underline m+\underline n=\underline{m+n}$: for $n=0$, this is the axiom $x+0=x$; then $\underline m+(\underline n+1)=(\underline m+\underline n)+1=\underline{m+n}+1$, which is the term $\underline{m+n+1}$. Likewise $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\underline m\times\underline n=\underline{mn}$. By induction on a closed term $t$ with value $n$, $\mathsf{PA}\vdash t=\underline n$.

(b) Differences. If $m<n$, $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\neg(\underline m=\underline n)$, by induction on $m$: $\underline n$ has the form $\underline k+1$, and one uses $\neg(x+1=0)$ for $m=0$, then $x+1=y+1\Rightarrow x=y$ and the induction hypothesis.

(c) Bounded quantifiers. $\mathsf{PA}$ proves by induction the usual rules of calculation (associativity and commutativity of $+$ and $\times$, distributivity, cancellation, $x=0\vee\exists w\,x=w+1$). One deduces, by induction on $n$, $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\forall x\,\big(x<\underline n\Leftrightarrow x=\underline 0\vee\cdots\vee x=\underline{n-1}\big)$ (for $n=0$, $\neg\,x<0$).

(d) Bounded sentences, by induction on their number of symbols. A closed equality $t_1=t_2$ reduces by (a) to $\underline m=\underline n$, provable if $m=n$ and refutable otherwise by (b). The connectives are handled by cases. For $\forall x<t\,\phi(x)$: if $t$ has value $n$, (a) and (c) show that the sentence is equivalent in $\mathsf{PA}$ to $\phi(\underline0)\wedge\cdots\wedge\phi(\underline{n-1})$, and each $\phi(\underline k)$, which has fewer symbols, is provable or refutable according to its truth. Likewise for $\exists x<t$.

(e) Σ sentences, by induction: the atomic cases, $\wedge$, $\vee$ and bounded quantifiers are as in (d); if $\exists y\,\phi(y)$ is true, $\phi(\underline m)$ is true for some $m$, hence provable, and $\exists y\,\phi(y)$ follows.

Lemma
PA knows the primitive recursive functions

For every primitive recursive function $f$, the formula $\phi_f(\vec x,y)$ built in the definability proposition satisfies $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\forall\vec x\,\exists y\,\phi_f(\vec x,y)$ and $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\forall\vec x\,\forall y\,\forall y'\,\big(\phi_f(\vec x,y)\wedge\phi_f(\vec x,y')\Rightarrow y=y'\big)$, and $\mathsf{PA}$ proves the equations defining $f$. In particular, if $f(\vec n)=m$, then $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\forall y\,\big(\phi_f(\underline{\vec n},y)\Leftrightarrow y=\underline m\big)$.

By induction on the definition of $f$. The base functions and substitution are immediate (the composition of functional relations is functional). For recursion $f=\rho[g,h]$, the only non-trivial point is the extension lemma, an internal version of the β lemma: $\mathsf{PA}$ proves that every coded sequence $(c,d)$ of length $n$ can be extended by an arbitrary value $a$, that is, $\forall c\,\forall d\,\forall n\,\forall a\,\exists c'\,\exists d'\,\big(\forall i<n\ \beta(c',d',i)=\beta(c,d,i)\ \wedge\ \beta(c',d',n)=a\big)$. It is proved in $\mathsf{PA}$ by following the proof of the β lemma, each step being an induction: the existence of a common multiple $d'$ of $1,\dots,s$ (induction on $s$) replaces $s!$, the coprimality of the moduli follows from Euclid’s lemma (which $\mathsf{PA}$ proves via the least number principle and Bézout’s identity), and the Chinese remainders are obtained by induction on the number of congruences (if $c$ works for the first $k$ moduli and $L$ is their product, coprime to $m_k$, one of the $c+jL$, $j<m_k$, also works for $m_k$). With this lemma, the existence of $y$ in $\phi_f(n,\vec x,y)$ is proved by induction on $n$ in $\mathsf{PA}$; uniqueness, by showing by induction on $i\le n$ that two witness sequences have the same terms (functionality of $\phi_g$ and $\phi_h$); and the equations $f(0,\vec x)=g(\vec x)$, $f(n+1,\vec x)=h(n,f(n,\vec x),\vec x)$ by extending the witness sequence by one term. The last claim combines uniqueness with the Σ-completeness lemma, which gives $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\phi_f(\underline{\vec n},\underline m)$.

Lemma
Diagonal lemma

For every formula $\psi(x)$ with one free variable, there is a sentence $G$ such that $\mathsf{PA}\vdash G\Leftrightarrow\psi(\underline{\ulcorner G\urcorner})$.

In plain words. For every property of sentences, one can write a sentence that asserts of itself that it has this property.

This is the logical version of programs that know their own text. With the property “not being provable”, we obtain Gödel’s sentence: “I am not provable”. If it were provable, it would be false, and the system would prove a falsehood; so it is not provable, and that is precisely what it says: it is true.

Let $\mathrm{sb}(m,n)$ be the code of the formula obtained by replacing, in the formula with code $m$, the variable $x$ by $\underline n$; $\mathrm{sb}$ is primitive recursive. Set $\delta(x)=\exists y\,\big(\phi_{\mathrm{sb}}(x,x,y)\wedge\psi(y)\big)$, $m=\ulcorner\delta\urcorner$ and $G=\delta(\underline m)$. Then $\mathrm{sb}(m,m)=\ulcorner G\urcorner=g$, and, by the previous lemma, $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\forall y\,\big(\phi_{\mathrm{sb}}(\underline m,\underline m,y)\Leftrightarrow y=\underline g\big)$. So $\mathsf{PA}\vdash G\Leftrightarrow\exists y\,(y=\underline g\wedge\psi(y))\Leftrightarrow\psi(\underline g)$.

Lemma
Derivability conditions (Hilbert, Bernays, Löb)

For every theory $\mathsf T$ as above and all sentences $\phi,\psi$:

  1. if $\mathsf T\vdash\phi$, then $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\Box\phi$;
  2. $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\Box(\phi\Rightarrow\psi)\Rightarrow(\Box\phi\Rightarrow\Box\psi)$;
  3. $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\sigma\Rightarrow\Box\sigma$ for every Σ sentence $\sigma$; in particular $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\Box\phi\Rightarrow\Box\Box\phi$.

In plain words. Peano arithmetic “knows” about proofs what we know ourselves: that a written proof is a proof, that two proofs can be combined, and that it can check its own proofs.

These three properties are the only ingredients of Gödel’s second theorem.

Notation. For a formula $\phi(\vec x)$, let $s_\phi(\vec n)=\ulcorner\phi(\underline{\vec n})\urcorner$, a primitive recursive function (substitution of the numerals $\underline{n_j}$, with code $\mathrm{num}(n_j)$, for the variables). We write $\Box\phi(\dot{\vec x})$ for the formula $\exists f\,\big(\phi_{s_\phi}(\vec x,f)\wedge\mathrm{Prov}_{\mathsf T}(f)\big)$; for closed $\phi$, this is $\Box\phi$. The primitive recursive relation “$p$ is a proof of $f$” is defined, by a formula $\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}$, as: $\mathrm{lh}(p)\ge1$, the last term of $p$ is $f$, and for every $i<\mathrm{lh}(p)$, the term $(p)_i$ is an axiom, or there are $j,k<i$ with $(p)_k=\mathrm{imp}((p)_j,(p)_i)$ (modus ponens), or there are $j<i$ and a variable $v$ with $(p)_i=\mathrm{gen}(v,(p)_j)$ (generalization); $\mathrm{imp}(a,b)$ is the code of $\phi\Rightarrow\psi$ if $a,b$ are those of $\phi,\psi$, and so on.

(F) Symbolic computation in PA. $\mathsf{PA}$ proves the defining equations of primitive recursive functions (lemma), hence all identities obtained by unfolding these definitions finitely many times, and, by induction, identities with variables such as $\mathrm{num}(y+1)=\ulcorner\cdot+1\urcorner(\mathrm{num}(y))$ (the numeral of $y+1$ is that of $y$ followed by $+1$), “$\mathrm{num}(y)$ is a closed term”, $s_{\phi\Rightarrow\psi}(\vec x)=\mathrm{imp}(s_\phi(\vec x),s_\psi(\vec x))$, or the lengths and terms of a concatenation of sequences: $\mathrm{lh}(p\frown q)=\mathrm{lh}(p)+\mathrm{lh}(q)$, $(p\frown q)_i=(p)_i$ if $i<\mathrm{lh}(p)$ and $(p\frown q)_{\mathrm{lh}(p)+i}=(q)_i$ if $i<\mathrm{lh}(q)$. Likewise, $\mathsf{PA}$ proves that $\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(p,f)$ is equivalent to the conjunction of the clauses defining proofs. These are the only facts about the coding used below.

(1) If $p$ codes a proof of $\phi$, the sentence $\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(\underline p,\underline{\ulcorner\phi\urcorner})$ is true, hence provable in $\mathsf{PA}$ (Σ-completeness), and $\Box\phi$ follows. We shall need the uniform form: if $\mathsf T\vdash\phi(\vec x)$, then $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\forall\vec x\,\Box\phi(\dot{\vec x})$. Let $\pi$ be a proof of $\phi(\vec x)$; a proof of $\phi(\underline{\vec n})$ is $\pi$ followed by the generalizations $\forall x_k\phi,\dots,\forall x_1\cdots\forall x_k\phi$, then the logical axioms $\forall x_1\cdots\forall x_k\phi\Rightarrow\forall x_2\cdots\forall x_k\phi(\underline{n_1},x_2,\dots)$, etc., and the corresponding modus ponens. Its code $P(\vec n)$ is a primitive recursive function of $\vec n$, and $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\forall\vec n\,\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(P(\vec n),s_\phi(\vec n))$: the lines of $\pi$ and the generalizations are fixed lines, whose correctness $\mathsf{PA}$ checks (Σ-completeness), and the instantiation lines are logical axioms because $\mathrm{num}(n_j)$ is a closed term (F), hence substitutable.

(2) Reason in $\mathsf{PA}$. Let $p$ be a proof of $\mathrm{imp}(a,b)$ and $q$ a proof of $a$, and $r=p\frown q\frown\langle b\rangle$. By (F), for $i<\mathrm{lh}(p)$, the line $(r)_i=(p)_i$ is justified in $r$ as in $p$, by the same indices; for $\mathrm{lh}(p)\le i<\mathrm{lh}(p)+\mathrm{lh}(q)$, the line $(r)_i=(q)_{i-\mathrm{lh}(p)}$ is justified as in $q$, the indices being shifted by $\mathrm{lh}(p)$; the last line $b$ is obtained by modus ponens from lines $\mathrm{lh}(p)+\mathrm{lh}(q)-1$ (which is $a$) and $\mathrm{lh}(p)-1$ (which is $\mathrm{imp}(a,b)$). So $\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(r,b)$. This gives (2), and its form with variables: $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\Box(\phi\Rightarrow\psi)(\dot{\vec x})\wedge\Box\phi(\dot{\vec x})\Rightarrow\Box\psi(\dot{\vec x})$, thanks to $s_{\phi\Rightarrow\psi}=\mathrm{imp}(s_\phi,s_\psi)$. With the uniform form of (1), one gets: if $\mathsf T\vdash\phi_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\phi_k\Rightarrow\psi$ (formulas with variables $\vec x$), then $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\Box\phi_1(\dot{\vec x})\wedge\cdots\wedge\Box\phi_k(\dot{\vec x})\Rightarrow\Box\psi(\dot{\vec x})$. Call this reasoning under $\Box$.

(3) We show, for every Σ formula $\sigma(\vec x)$, that $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\forall\vec x\,\big(\sigma(\vec x)\Rightarrow\Box\sigma(\dot{\vec x})\big)$, by induction on $\sigma$, after three preliminary results proved in $\mathsf{PA}$.

  • (A) $\forall u\,\forall v\ \Box(\dot u+\dot v=\dot w)$ with $w=u+v$. By induction on $v$: for $v=0$, reason under $\Box$ with the axiom $x+0=x$ ($\dot 0$ is the term $0$); from $v$ to $v+1$, the axiom $x+(y+1)=(x+y)+1$, the hypothesis $\Box(\dot u+\dot v=\dot w)$ and the rules of equality give, under $\Box$, $\dot u+(\dot v+1)=\dot w+1$, and $\dot v+1$, $\dot w+1$ are the numerals of $v+1$, $w+1$ (F). Likewise (M) for $\times$, with the axioms of $\times$ and (A).
  • (T) For every term $t(\vec x)$: $\forall\vec x\ \Box\big(t(\dot{\vec x})=\dot n\big)$ with $n=t(\vec x)$. By induction on $t$: $\Box(\dot x=\dot x)$; $0$ and $1$ are provably equal to the numerals $0$ and $0+1$; for $t_1+t_2$ and $t_1\times t_2$, reason under $\Box$ with the induction hypotheses, the compatibility of equality with $+$ and $\times$, then (A) or (M).
  • (N) $\forall u\,\forall v\ \big(u\ne v\Rightarrow\Box\neg(\dot u=\dot v)\big)$. By induction on $u$: if $u=0$, then $v=v_1+1$ and the axiom $\neg(x+1=0)$ gives $\Box\neg(0=\dot v_1+1)$; if $u=u_1+1$ and $v=0$, likewise; if $u=u_1+1$ and $v=v_1+1$, the induction hypothesis gives $\Box\neg(\dot u_1=\dot v_1)$, and the axiom $x+1=y+1\Rightarrow x=y$, under $\Box$, gives $\Box\neg(\dot u_1+1=\dot v_1+1)$.

Induction on $\sigma$, still in $\mathsf{PA}$:

  • $t_1=t_2$: if $t_1(\vec x)=t_2(\vec x)=n$, (T) gives $\Box(t_1(\dot{\vec x})=\dot n)$ and $\Box(t_2(\dot{\vec x})=\dot n)$, whence $\Box(t_1(\dot{\vec x})=t_2(\dot{\vec x}))$ under $\Box$. $\neg(t_1=t_2)$: if the values $n_1\ne n_2$, (T) and (N) likewise give $\Box\neg(t_1(\dot{\vec x})=t_2(\dot{\vec x}))$;
  • $\sigma_1\wedge\sigma_2$, $\sigma_1\vee\sigma_2$: induction hypotheses, then under $\Box$ with $\phi\Rightarrow(\psi\Rightarrow\phi\wedge\psi)$ and $\phi\Rightarrow\phi\vee\psi$;
  • $\exists y\,\sigma(\vec x,y)$: if $\sigma(\vec x,y)$, the induction hypothesis (the variable $y$ being one of the variables) gives $\Box\sigma(\dot{\vec x},\dot y)$, and the logical axiom $\sigma(\vec x,y)\Rightarrow\exists y\,\sigma(\vec x,y)$, under $\Box$, gives $\Box\,\exists y\,\sigma(\dot{\vec x},y)$;
  • $\forall y<t\ \sigma$: first show, by induction on $n$, $\forall y<n\ \sigma(\vec x,y)\Rightarrow\Box\,\forall y<\dot n\ \sigma(\dot{\vec x},y)$. For $n=0$, $\forall y<0\ \sigma$ is provable ($\neg\,y<0$). From $n$ to $n+1$: the hypothesis gives $\forall y<n\ \sigma$ and $\sigma(\vec x,n)$, hence $\Box\,\forall y<\dot n\ \sigma$ (induction) and $\Box\sigma(\dot{\vec x},\dot n)$ (induction on $\sigma$), and the provable formula $\forall y<z\ \sigma\wedge\sigma(\vec x,z)\Rightarrow\forall y<z+1\ \sigma$, under $\Box$, concludes. One passes from $\dot n$ to the term $t$ by (T). The case $\exists y<t\ \sigma$ is similar: a witness $y<n$ gives $\Box\sigma(\dot{\vec x},\dot y)$ and $\Box(\dot y<\dot n)$ (cases already treated, $y<n$ being a Σ formula).

Finally $\Box\phi$ is a Σ sentence, whence $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\Box\phi\Rightarrow\Box\Box\phi$.

The proofs above are complete, except that the symbol-by-symbol writing of the coding functions and of the identities (F) is not spelled out: these are primitive recursive definitions and routine inductions (Hilbert and Bernays, 1939, give all the details; see also Boolos, The Logic of Provability, 1993).

Theorem
Second incompleteness theorem (Gödel, 1931)

Let $\mathsf T$ be a theory containing $\mathsf{PA}$ whose set of axioms is primitive recursive. If $\mathsf T$ is consistent, then $\mathsf T$ does not prove the sentence $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf T)$ expressing its own consistency.

In plain words. A reasonable system of axioms that contains arithmetic cannot prove that it never leads to a contradiction, unless it is precisely contradictory.

A system cannot guarantee its own reliability from within. To prove that a theory is consistent, one needs a stronger theory, whose consistency is even more doubtful. This is the end of Hilbert’s programme: mathematics cannot found itself in an absolutely certain way. It is none the worse for it; like all knowledge, it rests on a reasoned trust.

By the diagonal lemma applied to $\psi(x)=\neg\mathrm{Prov}_{\mathsf T}(x)$, there is $G$ with $\mathsf{PA}\vdash G\Leftrightarrow\neg\Box G$: “I am not provable in $\mathsf T$”.

(i) $\mathsf T\nvdash G$. Otherwise, by condition 1, $\mathsf T\vdash\Box G$, and, since $\mathsf T\vdash G\Rightarrow\neg\Box G$, $\mathsf T\vdash\neg\Box G$: $\mathsf T$ would be inconsistent.

(ii) $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf T)\Rightarrow G$. Indeed:

  • from $\mathsf{PA}\vdash G\Rightarrow\neg\Box G$, conditions 1 and 2 give $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\Box G\Rightarrow\Box\neg\Box G$;
  • condition 3 gives $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\Box G\Rightarrow\Box\Box G$;
  • the tautology $\Box G\Rightarrow(\neg\Box G\Rightarrow0=1)$ and conditions 1 and 2 give $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\Box\Box G\Rightarrow(\Box\neg\Box G\Rightarrow\Box(0=1))$;
  • so $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\Box G\Rightarrow\Box(0=1)$, that is, $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf T)\Rightarrow\neg\Box G$, and $\neg\Box G\Rightarrow G$.

(iii) If $\mathsf T\vdash\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf T)$, then, since $\mathsf T$ contains $\mathsf{PA}$, (ii) gives $\mathsf T\vdash G$, which (i) forbids.

Point (ii) is the formalization, in $\mathsf{PA}$, of the first theorem: “if $\mathsf T$ is consistent, $G$ is not provable”. All the technical difficulty lies in condition 3.

Theorem
Gödel under ω-consistency and Rosser under consistency

Let $\mathsf T$ be a theory containing $\mathsf{PA}$, with a primitive recursive set of axioms.

  1. (Gödel, 1931) If $\mathsf T$ is ω-consistent, that is, if there is no formula $\phi(x)$ such that $\mathsf T\vdash\exists x\,\phi(x)$ and $\mathsf T\vdash\neg\phi(\underline n)$ for every $n$, then the sentence $G$ of the second theorem is neither provable nor refutable in $\mathsf T$.
  2. (Rosser, 1936) If $\mathsf T$ is merely consistent, there is a sentence $R$ that is neither provable nor refutable in $\mathsf T$.

In plain words. To obtain incompleteness, it is enough to assume that the system is not contradictory; there is no need to assume that it proves only truths.

This version, due to Barkley Rosser (1936), weakens the hypothesis to the minimum: a consistent system that contains arithmetic always leaves questions unanswered.

Recall that $\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(p,f)$ defines a primitive recursive relation: by the lemma on primitive recursive functions, for all integers $p,f$, $\mathsf{PA}$ proves $\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(\underline p,\underline f)$ if it is true and its negation otherwise. An ω-consistent theory is consistent (an inconsistent theory proves everything).

(1) $\mathsf T\nvdash G$ by point (i) of the proof of the second theorem (which uses only consistency). Suppose $\mathsf T\vdash\neg G$, hence $\mathsf T\vdash\Box G$, that is, $\mathsf T\vdash\exists p\ \mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(p,\underline{\ulcorner G\urcorner})$. Since $G$ has no proof, each sentence $\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(\underline n,\underline{\ulcorner G\urcorner})$ is false, hence refutable in $\mathsf{PA}\subseteq\mathsf T$: $\mathsf T$ is not ω-consistent.

(2) Let $\mathrm{neg}$ be the primitive recursive function that associates with $\ulcorner\phi\urcorner$ the code of $\neg\phi$. By the diagonal lemma, there is $R$ such that $$\mathsf{PA}\vdash R\Leftrightarrow\forall p\,\Big(\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(p,\underline{\ulcorner R\urcorner})\Rightarrow\exists q\le p\ \mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}\big(q,\mathrm{neg}(\underline{\ulcorner R\urcorner})\big)\Big),$$ where $\mathrm{neg}(\underline{\ulcorner R\urcorner})$ is written using the formula for $\mathrm{neg}$; $\mathsf{PA}$ proves that it equals $\underline{\ulcorner\neg R\urcorner}$.

  • Suppose $p_0$ codes a proof of $R$ in $\mathsf T$. Since $\mathsf T$ is consistent, $\neg R$ has no proof, and for each $q\le p_0$, $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\neg\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(\underline q,\underline{\ulcorner\neg R\urcorner})$. With $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\forall q\,(q\le\underline{p_0}\Leftrightarrow q=\underline0\vee\cdots\vee q=\underline{p_0})$ (Σ-completeness, point (c)), $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\neg\exists q\le\underline{p_0}\ \mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(q,\underline{\ulcorner\neg R\urcorner})$. With $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(\underline{p_0},\underline{\ulcorner R\urcorner})$, we get $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\neg R$, and $\mathsf T$ proves $R$ and $\neg R$: contradiction.
  • Suppose $q_0$ codes a proof of $\neg R$. Since $R$ has no proof, for each $p\le q_0$, $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\neg\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(\underline p,\underline{\ulcorner R\urcorner})$. Reason in $\mathsf{PA}$: let $p$ be such that $\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(p,\underline{\ulcorner R\urcorner})$; we have $p\le\underline{q_0}$ or $\underline{q_0}\le p$; the first case is excluded (it is one of $p=\underline0,\dots,\underline{q_0}$, all refuted); in the second, $q=\underline{q_0}$ works, since $\mathrm{Prf}_{\mathsf T}(\underline{q_0},\underline{\ulcorner\neg R\urcorner})$. So $\mathsf{PA}\vdash R$, and $\mathsf T$ proves $R$ and $\neg R$: contradiction.

The decision problem

Hilbert asked one last question: is there a mechanical method that says, for every logical statement, whether it is always true? Turing invented his machines precisely to answer it, and the answer is no.

We consider here predicate calculus without equality, over an arbitrary countable language: constants, function symbols and relation symbols. A sentence is valid if it is true in every structure (every nonempty domain, every interpretation of the symbols). Every sentence can be written, effectively, in negation normal form: without $\Rightarrow$, negation applying only to atomic formulas (push negations inwards with De Morgan’s laws and $\neg\forall=\exists\neg$). A sentence $\phi$ is valid if and only if the negation normal form of $\neg\phi$ has no model.

Theorem
Completeness of tableaux (Gödel, 1929, in the form of Beth and Smullyan)

A tableau for a finite set $\Gamma$ of sentences in negation normal form is a tree each node of which carries a sentence, built from a branch formed by the elements of $\Gamma$ by applying, to a sentence already present on a branch, one of the following rules at the end of that branch:

  • (α) for $A\wedge B$, add $A$ then $B$;
  • (β) for $A\vee B$, split the branch in two, one with $A$, the other with $B$;
  • (γ) for $\forall x\,A$, add $A[x:=t]$ for a closed term $t$;
  • (δ) for $\exists x\,A$, add $A[x:=c]$ for a new constant $c$, taken from a reserve $c_0,c_1,\dots$ and absent from the branch.

A branch is closed if it contains an atomic formula and its negation; a tableau is closed if all its branches are.

Theorem. $\Gamma$ has no model if and only if it admits a closed tableau, and one can then be found by a fixed systematic construction. Consequently, the set of valid sentences is semi-decidable.

In plain words. If a logical statement is true in every imaginable situation, there is a proof of it, and a machine can find it by trying all possible proofs.

This theorem of Gödel’s (1929), not to be confused with incompleteness, is reassuring: logic itself lets nothing escape. But the search stops only if the statement is true; if it is not, it may go on forever.

Soundness. If $\Gamma$ has a model $\mathcal M$, every tableau for $\Gamma$ has a branch all of whose sentences are true in an extension of $\mathcal M$ (interpreting the reserve constants used). This holds at the start, and each rule preserves it: for (β), one of $A$, $B$ is true; for (γ), $A[x:=t]$ is true since $\forall x\,A$ is; for (δ), interpret the new constant $c$ by a witness of $\exists x\,A$. Such a branch is not closed.

Systematic construction. Enumerate $t_0,t_1,\dots$ the closed terms of the language augmented with the reserve constants. At step $n$, on each non-closed branch, apply the rule of each sentence present: only once in total for α, β and δ; and, for each sentence $\forall x\,A$, add the $A[x:=t_k]$ for all $k\le n$ (those not already there). Stop extending a branch as soon as it is closed. Each step is finite.

König’s lemma. If the systematic tableau is closed, it is finite. Indeed, each node has at most two children. If the tree were infinite, one of the children of the root would have infinitely many descendants, then one of its children, and so on: one would obtain an infinite branch. But a closed branch stops being extended, so an infinite branch is never closed.

Completeness. If the systematic tableau is not closed, it has an open branch $B$ (finite, or infinite by König’s lemma). Let $H$ be the set of sentences of $B$. By construction:

  • $H$ does not contain both an atom and its negation;
  • if $A\wedge B\in H$, then $A,B\in H$, and if $A\vee B\in H$, then $A\in H$ or $B\in H$;
  • if $\exists x\,A\in H$, then $A[x:=c]\in H$ for some constant $c$;
  • if $\forall x\,A\in H$, then $A[x:=t]\in H$ for every closed term $t$, since $t=t_k$ is added at step $\max(n,k)$ if $\forall x\,A$ appears at step $n$.

Define the Herbrand structure $\mathcal H$: its domain is the set of closed terms; a function symbol $f$ sends $t_1,\dots,t_k$ to the term $f(t_1,\dots,t_k)$; a relation $P$ is true at $(t_1,\dots,t_k)$ if and only if $P(t_1,\dots,t_k)\in H$. Each closed term denotes itself there, and, by induction on the size of sentences, every sentence of $H$ is true in $\mathcal H$: for an atom, this is the definition; for $\neg P(\vec t)$, use that $P(\vec t)\notin H$; for $\forall x\,A$, all its instances $A[x:=t]$, smaller, are true, and the elements of the domain are exactly the terms $t$. So $\Gamma\subseteq H$ has a model.

Semi-decidability. For a sentence $\phi$, build step by step the systematic tableau of the negation normal form of $\neg\phi$, and stop if it closes. By the above, this happens if and only if $\neg\phi$ has no model, that is, if $\phi$ is valid.

With equality, one reduces to this case by replacing $=$ by a relation symbol $E$ and adding as hypotheses the axioms making $E$ a congruence; a model of these axioms gives, by passing to the quotient, a model in which $E$ is equality.

Example: $\phi=\exists x\,\forall y\,P(x,y)\Rightarrow\forall y\,\exists x\,P(x,y)$. The negation normal form of $\neg\phi$ is $\exists x\,\forall y\,P(x,y)\wedge\exists y\,\forall x\,\neg P(x,y)$. The tableau: (α) gives the two conjuncts; (δ) gives $\forall y\,P(c_0,y)$ and $\forall x\,\neg P(x,c_1)$; (γ) gives $P(c_0,c_1)$ and $\neg P(c_0,c_1)$: the branch is closed, so $\phi$ is valid. The converse $\forall y\,\exists x\,P\Rightarrow\exists x\,\forall y\,P$ gives on the contrary an infinite tableau, whose open branch describes a countermodel.

Theorem
Church-Turing theorem (1936)

There is no algorithm that decides whether a sentence of first-order predicate calculus is universally valid (true in all interpretations), even without equality. Hilbert and Ackermann’s Entscheidungsproblem has a negative answer. The set of valid sentences is semi-decidable, but not decidable.

In plain words. There is no mechanical method that says, for every logical statement, whether it is true in all situations.

This is the question Hilbert called the “decision problem”, and it was to answer it that Turing invented his machines in 1936. There is no “proving machine” that settles all questions: mathematical reasoning does not reduce to a computation that always ends.

To every machine $Z$ (in quadruple format) and every integer $x$, we effectively associate a sentence $\phi_{Z,x}$, valid if and only if $Z$ halts on input $\overline x$. A decider for validity would therefore decide halting, which is impossible; and semi-decidability is the completeness theorem.

The language. A constant $o$, two function symbols $s$ and $p$ (successor and predecessor), two binary relations $E$ (“same cell”) and $L$ (“to the left of”), and, for each state $q_i$ and each symbol $S_j$ of $Z$, two binary relations: $Q_i(t,h)$, “at instant $t$, the machine is in state $q_i$, the head at $h$”, and $C_j(t,u)$, “at instant $t$, cell $u$ contains $S_j$”. We write $[z]$, for $z\in\mathbb Z$, for the term $s^z(o)$ if $z\ge0$ and $p^{-z}(o)$ if $z<0$. The instants will be the terms $s^k(o)$.

The axioms (universal closures):

  • (E) $E$ is an equivalence relation; $E(x,y)\Rightarrow E(sx,sy)\wedge E(px,py)$; $E(s\,p\,x,x)$; $E(p\,s\,x,x)$; $E(x,y)\wedge Q_i(t,x)\Rightarrow Q_i(t,y)$ and $E(x,y)\wedge C_j(t,x)\Rightarrow C_j(t,y)$ for all $i,j$;
  • (L) $L(x,sx)$; $L(x,y)\wedge L(y,z)\Rightarrow L(x,z)$; $\neg L(x,x)$; $E(x,y)\wedge L(y,z)\Rightarrow L(x,z)$; $L(x,y)\wedge E(y,z)\Rightarrow L(x,z)$;
  • (I) the initial state: $Q_0(o,o)$, $C_1(o,[k])$ for $0\le k\le x$, and $L(u,o)\vee L([x],u)\Rightarrow C_B(o,u)$;
  • (T) for each quadruple $q_iS_jS_kq_l$: $Q_i(t,h)\wedge C_j(t,h)\Rightarrow Q_l(st,h)\wedge C_k(st,h)$; for $q_iS_jRq_l$: $Q_i(t,h)\wedge C_j(t,h)\Rightarrow Q_l(st,sh)\wedge C_j(st,h)$; for $q_iS_jLq_l$: likewise with $ph$;
  • (F) for each quadruple beginning with $q_iS_j$ and each symbol $S_m$: $Q_i(t,h)\wedge C_j(t,h)\wedge C_m(t,u)\wedge\neg E(u,h)\Rightarrow C_m(st,u)$ (the other cells do not change).

Let $\mathrm{Ax}$ be their conjunction, and $\mathrm{Halt}=\exists t\,\exists h\,\bigvee Q_i(t,h)\wedge C_j(t,h)$, the disjunction ranging over the pairs $(q_i,S_j)$ for which $Z$ has no quadruple. Set $\phi_{Z,x}=\mathrm{Ax}\Rightarrow\mathrm{Halt}$.

If $Z$ halts, $\phi_{Z,x}$ is valid. Let $\mathcal M$ be a model of $\mathrm{Ax}$, and $e_z$ the element denoted by $[z]$. By (E), $s(e_z)$ is $E$-equivalent to $e_{z+1}$ and $p(e_z)$ to $e_{z-1}$ (for example, for $z<0$, $s(e_z)=s(p(e_{z+1}))$). By (L), $L(e_z,e_{z'})$ for $z<z'$; so $\neg E(e_z,e_{z'})$ if $z\neq z'$, since $E(e_{z'},e_z)$ and $L(e_z,e_{z'})$ would give $L(e_{z'},e_{z'})$. We then show, by induction on $k$: if, after $k$ steps, the machine is in state $q_i$, the head on cell $z$, with contents $\tau(w)$ in each cell $w$, then $\mathcal M\models Q_i(s^ko,e_z)$ and $\mathcal M\models C_{\tau(w)}(s^ko,e_w)$ for every $w\in\mathbb Z$. For $k=0$, this is (I) (the cells $w<0$ and $w>x$ satisfy $L(e_w,o)$ or $L(e_x,e_w)$). At the next step, the axiom (T) of the quadruple applied gives the new state, at a position $E$-equivalent to $e_{z\pm1}$ or $e_z$, then (E) transports it to $e_{z\pm1}$; it also gives the contents of cell $z$; and (F) gives those of the cells $w\ne z$, since $\neg E(e_w,e_z)$. If the machine halts after $k$ steps in state $q_i$ reading $S_j$, no quadruple begins with $q_iS_j$, and $t=s^ko$, $h=e_z$ witness $\mathrm{Halt}$.

If $Z$ does not halt, $\phi_{Z,x}$ is not valid. Take the domain $\mathbb Z$, $o=0$, $s(z)=z+1$, $p(z)=z-1$, $E$ equality, $L$ the strict order, $Q_i(t,h)$ true if and only if $t\ge0$ and the machine is, after $t$ steps, in state $q_i$ with the head at $h$, and $C_j(t,u)$ true if and only if $t\ge0$ and cell $u$ then contains $S_j$. The axioms (E), (L), (I) are true. For (T) and (F): their premise $Q_i(t,h)\wedge C_j(t,h)$ is true only if, at instant $t$, the machine is in state $q_i$ and reads $S_j$; since $Z$ is deterministic, the quadruple beginning with $q_iS_j$ is the one that applies, and the conclusion describes instant $t+1$. Finally $\mathrm{Halt}$ is false, since the machine never halts. This structure is a model of $\mathrm{Ax}\wedge\neg\mathrm{Halt}$.

The map $(Z,x)\mapsto\phi_{Z,x}$ is computable, and $\phi_{Z,x}$ does not contain equality. The halting problem therefore reduces to validity, which is undecidable.

Exercises

Levels: ★ direct application of the course, ★★ standard exercise, ★★★ going further. Solutions are hidden: click “Solution” to show them.

★Exercise 1 — 2 + 2 = 4 in PA

Show that $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\underline2+\underline2=\underline4$, using only the axioms $x+0=x$ and $x+(y+1)=(x+y)+1$ and the rules of equality.

Solution

Recall that $\underline2=(0+1)+1$ and $\underline4=(((0+1)+1)+1)+1$. By the axiom $x+(y+1)=(x+y)+1$ with $y=0+1$: $\underline2+\underline2=\underline2+((0+1)+1)=(\underline2+(0+1))+1$. Again with $y=0$: $\underline2+(0+1)=(\underline2+0)+1$, and $\underline2+0=\underline2$ by the first axiom. Substituting (rules of equality): $\underline2+\underline2=((\underline2)+1)+1=\underline4$. Each step is an instance of an axiom or of the substitution rule; this is the prototype of Σ-completeness: true computations on explicit integers are provable.

★Exercise 2 — A complete theory is decidable

Let $\mathsf T$ be a consistent theory with decidable axioms, complete: for every sentence $\phi$, $\mathsf T\vdash\phi$ or $\mathsf T\vdash\neg\phi$. Show that the set of theorems of $\mathsf T$ is decidable.

Solution

Given $\phi$, enumerate all proofs of $\mathsf T$ (one can check mechanically whether a text is a proof). By completeness, one eventually finds a proof of $\phi$ or a proof of $\neg\phi$. In the first case, $\phi$ is a theorem. In the second, $\phi$ is not one, otherwise $\mathsf T$ would prove $\phi$ and $\neg\phi$, and would be inconsistent. The procedure always terminates. Contrapositive: a consistent theory with decidable axioms whose theorems are undecidable is incomplete. This is the road to incompleteness followed in exercise 5.

★★Exercise 3 — The theorems of PA are undecidable

Assume that $\mathsf{PA}$ is sound. Show that $K\le_m\{\ulcorner\phi\urcorner:\mathsf{PA}\vdash\phi\}$, and deduce that the set of theorems of $\mathsf{PA}$ is semi-decidable but not decidable.

Solution

By definability, one computes from $e$ a $\Sigma_1$ sentence $H_e$ expressing “$\varphi_e(e)\conv$” (“there is a computation code $s$ such that $T(e,e,s)$”). If $e\in K$, $H_e$ is a true Σ sentence, hence provable in $\mathsf{PA}$ (Σ-completeness). If $\mathsf{PA}\vdash H_e$, then $H_e$ is true, since $\mathsf{PA}$ is sound, so $e\in K$. Thus $e\mapsto\ulcorner H_e\urcorner$ reduces $K$ to the theorems. These are semi-decidable (enumerate proofs) and not decidable, otherwise $K$ would be.

★★Exercise 4 — Consistent but false

Assume $\mathsf{PA}$ is consistent. Show that the theory $\mathsf T=\mathsf{PA}+\neg\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ is consistent, but that it proves a false sentence.

Solution

If $\mathsf T$ were inconsistent, we would have $\mathsf{PA}+\neg\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})\vdash0=1$, so $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\neg\neg\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ (proof by contradiction, valid in the calculus used), that is, $\mathsf{PA}\vdash\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, which the second incompleteness theorem forbids. So $\mathsf T$ is consistent. But it proves $\neg\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, which says “there is a proof of $0=1$ in $\mathsf{PA}$”: this is false since $\mathsf{PA}$ is consistent. Consistency is therefore strictly weaker than soundness, and this is why Rosser had to work to use consistency alone (Rosser’s theorem). $\mathsf T$ asserts the existence of an integer (the code of a proof) that it cannot exhibit: it is not ω-consistent.

★★★Exercise 5 — Every consistent extension of PA is undecidable

Let $\mathsf T\supseteq\mathsf{PA}$ be consistent, not necessarily sound. Using the recursively inseparable sets $A=\{e:\varphi_e(e)=0\}$ and $B=\{e:\varphi_e(e)=1\}$, show that the set of theorems of $\mathsf T$ is not decidable. Deduce a new proof of incompleteness under the sole hypothesis of consistency.

Solution

Write “$\varphi_e(e)=y$” in the form $V_e(y)$: $\exists s\,\big(T(e,e,s)\wedge U(s)=y\wedge\forall s'<s\,\neg T(e,e,s')\big)$, with $T$ and $U$ represented as in the definability proposition. Set $C=\{e:\mathsf T\vdash V_e(\underline0)\}$.

$A\subseteq C$. If $\varphi_e(e)=0$, $V_e(\underline0)$ is a true Σ sentence, hence provable in $\mathsf{PA}\subseteq\mathsf T$.

$B\cap C=\emptyset$. If $\varphi_e(e)=1$, with computation coded by $s_0$, then $\mathsf{PA}$ proves $\neg V_e(\underline0)$. Indeed, reason in $\mathsf{PA}$ about an $s$ witnessing $V_e(\underline0)$. If $s<\underline{s_0}$, $s$ is one of the numerals $\underline0,\dots,\underline{s_0-1}$, and $\neg T(e,e,\underline j)$ is provable for each (true bounded sentence). If $s=\underline{s_0}$, $U(\underline{s_0})=\underline1\ne\underline0$. If $s>\underline{s_0}$, the clause $\forall s'<s\,\neg T$ is contradicted at $s'=\underline{s_0}$. So $\mathsf T\vdash\neg V_e(\underline0)$, and by consistency $\mathsf T\nvdash V_e(\underline0)$: $e\notin C$.

If the theorems of $\mathsf T$ were decidable, $C$ would be decidable and would separate $A$ from $B$: impossible. With exercise 2, if the axioms of $\mathsf T$ are decidable, $\mathsf T$ is incomplete. We have recovered Rosser’s theorem without building a self-referential sentence.
