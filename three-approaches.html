Three approaches to computation: imperative, functional, logical

Modifying a memory, rewriting an expression, defining a function: three ways of thinking about computation, at the origin of the three great families of programming languages.

Three families of models

There are several ways of describing a computation. We can see it as a sequence of actions on a memory (“put 3 in box A, add 1”), as the step-by-step simplification of an expression (as one reduces a fraction), or as the definition of a function from simpler functions. These three points of view gave birth to different mathematical models, and also to different programming languages.

There are several ways of specifying a computation. Three families dominate:

  • The imperative (action-based) approach: computation proceeds by modifying a memory over time; the result is read off the final state of the memory. Canonical model: the Turing machine.
  • The functional approach: computation transforms one expression into another, by rewriting rules, until no rule applies; the final expression is the result. Canonical models: the λ-calculus and rewriting systems.
  • The mathematical or logical approach: one describes which functions are acceptable, by composition, recursion and search, without describing any execution. Canonical model: the recursive functions.

These three families are at the origin of imperative (C, Python), functional (Lisp, OCaml, Haskell) and logic (Prolog, Datalog) languages. Modern languages mix these influences, but they can still be recognized. The central result of the first part of this manual is that these three approaches compute exactly the same functions.

The imperative approach

This is the machine’s point of view: a memory, and instructions that modify it step by step. The Turing machine, computer processors and languages such as C or Python belong to it. We speak of “imperative” programming: the program gives orders.

This approach is both very intuitive and very “technological”: computing a function is not a primitive activity here, but a side effect of modifying a memory. The Turing machine model (1936) rests on an automaton that reads and writes symbols on a tape. Turing explicitly designed it by analysing what a human does when computing with a pencil on squared paper: they look at only a bounded number of squares at a time, they have only finitely many “states of mind”, and each action modifies one square or shifts their attention by one square.

The tape representation has no particular significance; it does show, however, that a non-trivial interpretation step is needed between the internal activity of a machine and the communication of its results. Peripherals (screen, printer, network) turn the 0s and 1s of memory into perceptible phenomena: an image is nothing but the display of a gigantic number describing, for each pixel, three intensities of red, green and blue.

This approach has a major advantage: it is close to what happens in a real machine. The notion of computation time is realistic, and complexity classes are built on it. It also has a cost: one reasons about a translation of the function rather than about the function itself, which makes correctness proofs difficult.

Proposition
Cost of simulation between imperative models

(1) A $k$-tape Turing machine that halts in $t$ steps on an input of length $n$ is simulated by a one-tape machine in $O\big((n+t)^2\big)$ steps, i.e. $O(t^2)$ if $t\ge n$.

(2) A random access machine (RAM model, defined below) executing $t$ instructions on an input of $n$ binary digits is simulated by a multi-tape Turing machine in $O\big(t^2(t+n)\big)$ steps, i.e. $O(t^3)$ if $t\ge n$.

These overheads are polynomial, in general not constant.

In plain words. There are several kinds of machines: with a single tape, with several tapes, or with a memory in which any cell can be reached directly, like a computer. They can all do exactly the same computations. Each can imitate the others, only going a little more slowly.

“A little more slowly” has a precise meaning: if a computation takes a thousand steps on one, it will take at worst a million or a billion on the other, but never a number that explodes like 2 multiplied by itself a thousand times. The slowdown is said to be polynomial.

This robustness has an important consequence. We can separate the problems that are “feasible in practice” from the “infeasible” ones, and this classification does not depend on the machine chosen. This is called the extended Church-Turing thesis, discussed in the chapter Church’s thesis. The quantum computer is today the main candidate for refuting it.

(1) From $k$ tapes to one. A $k$-tape machine reads the $k$ symbols under its heads and, depending on its state, writes $k$ symbols, moves each head one square to the left, to the right or not at all, and changes state; the input is on the first tape, with the heads initially on square $0$. The one-tape machine $M'$ (with quintuples, which suffices, theorem) uses the product alphabet: the symbol of a square is a $2k$-tuple giving, for each tape $i$, the symbol of square $i$ and a flag “head $i$ is here”, plus a flag “left end of the visited zone”. The blank of $M'$ is read as the blank $2k$-tuple with no flag.

  • Preparation: in one pass over the input, $M'$ replaces each symbol $a$ by the $2k$-tuple $(a,B,\dots,B)$ and sets the head flags and the end flag on square $0$, then comes back: $O(n)$ steps.
  • One step of $M$: starting from the left end, $M'$ moves right until it has met the $k$ head flags, remembering in its state the $k$ symbols read (there are finitely many possibilities). It then knows the transition of $M$. It moves back left to the end; when passing over the flag of head $i$, it writes the new symbol of track $i$ and moves the flag: to the left, onto the square it is going to anyway; to the right, by a one-square round trip. If a flag leaves the zone on the left, the end flag follows.
  • End: when $M$ halts, $M'$ replaces each $2k$-tuple by its first-track symbol and places its head on the flag of head $1$.

After $s$ steps of $M$, each head lies in the interval $[-s,\,n-1+s]$, of length $n+2s$: each simulated step costs $O(n+t)$ steps of $M'$ (two passes and at most $2k$ round trips). The total is $O(n)+t\cdot O(n+t)+O(n+t)=O\big((n+t)^2\big)$. This is the argument of Hartmanis and Stearns (1965).

(2) Random access machines. A RAM has registers $R_0,R_1,R_2,\dots$ holding natural numbers and a finite program of instructions: $R_i:=c$ (constant); $R_i:=R_j+R_k$; $R_i:=R_j\dot-R_k$ (truncated subtraction); $R_i:=R_{R_j}$ and $R_{R_j}:=R_i$ (indirect addressing); “if $R_i=0$, go to instruction $\ell$”; halt. The inputs are in $R_1,\dots,R_p$ (with $n$ binary digits in all, and $p\le n$), the other registers hold $0$, the result is in $R_0$. Each instruction counts as one step.

Size of the numbers. Let $c_0$ be the number of digits of the largest constant in the program. An addition increases the number of digits of the largest register by at most one, the other instructions do not increase it: after $t$ steps, all contents, and hence all addresses used, have at most $b=n+c_0+t$ binary digits.

Simulation. The Turing machine $M$ has a “memory” tape holding a list of entries $\#\,a\,{:}\,v$, address and content in binary, one for each nonzero register already written, and a few work tapes. The number of the current instruction is kept in its state. To read $R_a$, $M$ writes $a$ on a work tape and scans the memory comparing each address with $a$, digit by digit; if the address is absent, $R_a=0$. To write $R_a:=v$, $M$ erases (by marking it “dead”) the entry with address $a$ if there is one and appends $\#\,a\,{:}\,v$ at the end of the list: the memory never needs to be shifted. There are at most $p+t$ entries, each of length $O(b)$, so a scan costs $O\big((p+t)\,b\big)$. An instruction requires a bounded number of reads and writes, and an addition, a subtraction or a test on numbers of $b$ digits, which take $O(b)$ steps with two tapes. The total is $$t\cdot O\big((p+t)\,b\big)=O\big(t\,(n+t)\,(n+t)\big),$$ which is $O\big(t^2(t+n)\big)$ when $t\ge n$ and also, in all cases, $O\big((n+t)^3\big)$. With (1), one goes down to a single tape at the price of a square. (See also Papadimitriou, Computational Complexity, 1994, Theorem 2.5.) If multiplication in one step is allowed, numbers can double in length at each step ($R_0:=R_0\times R_0$ repeated $t$ times gives $2^t$ digits): the overhead is no longer polynomial in $t$, which is why the “reasonable” RAM model is restricted to addition and subtraction, or charges operations according to the size of the numbers.

The phenomenon can be seen on the compiler of chapter 6: the Turing machine it produces for multiplication performs tens of thousands of steps to compute $4\times 4$, because each copy of a block of strokes requires round trips on the tape. A processor performs the same multiplication in one cycle. The gap is not just a constant: it grows with the size of the numbers.

Paradoxically, this closeness to the hardware is also a weakness: a hardware change (machine word size, byte order) can alter the meaning of a program and introduce errors that are not errors of reasoning.

The functional approach

This is the point of view of algebra: we compute by replacing an expression with a simpler equivalent expression, until nothing more can be simplified. The λ-calculus and languages such as OCaml or Haskell belong to it.

This approach lies halfway between machines and mathematical definitions. Computation is a sequence of syntactic transformations, like those learned at school with algebraic identities: the rule $(a+b)^2 \to a^2+2ab+b^2$ transforms an expression into an equivalent one. The other basic mechanism is substitution: the function $f$ is represented by the expression $x+1$; to compute $f(4)$, one replaces $x$ by $4$ and then applies the computation rules.

One no longer indicates, at each moment, which operation to perform. To evaluate $5+8+(4\times 5)$, one may start with $5+8$ or with $4\times 5$: the result is the same, provided the system is confluent (this is a theorem for the λ-calculus, see Confluence). The programmer specifies what to compute, not how: this is called declarative programming.

A functional program lends itself better to mathematical analysis: a property true of each rule is an invariant of the program, and reasoning is compositional. In imperative style, by contrast, global variables and pointers mean that an invariant true of two blocks taken separately may be false of their combination: the semicolon is not commutative.

The functional style also naturally handles higher order: programs that take or return programs, such as a compiler, or a function applying a transformation to every element of a list. In return, it hides important implementation details: if $f(x)=t$ where $x$ occurs a million times in $t$, the cost of $f(u)$ depends on how substitution is implemented, whereas in the λ-calculus it counts as a single reduction step.

The mathematical and logical approach

This is the mathematician’s point of view: a function is defined from functions already known, or a problem is described by logical rules, and we look for what follows from them. Recursive functions and languages such as Prolog belong to it.

This approach formalizes which functions are acceptable rather than describing a mechanism. Its main representative is the formalism of recursive functions, which systematizes definition by recursion: a function is well defined if its value at $0$ is given and if, knowing $f(n)$, one can compute $f(n+1)$ by operations already accepted. The basic operations are very simple: constants, adding 1, selecting an argument, composition.

Historically, the first version, today called primitive recursion, only yields total functions. It was shown not to be enough: the Ackermann function is total and computable, but not primitive recursive. The decisive extension is unbounded minimization: searching for the least $n$ such that $g(n)=0$, by testing $g(0)$, $g(1)$, … The search may never succeed: this is the mathematical version of the while loop, and the source of partiality, which is unavoidable, as we saw in the motivations.
