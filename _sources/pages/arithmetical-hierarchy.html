The halting problem cannot be solved by a program. But there are problems that are harder still: even an oracle that knew all the answers about halting would not be enough to solve them. And above them, still others. This chapter describes this infinite ladder of difficulties.

The surprise is that the position of a problem on the ladder can be read off from the way it is stated. “There is a number such that…” is confirmed by finding the number: this is the first rung. “For every number, there is a number such that…” is harder: this is the second. Each alternation between “there exists” and “for all” climbs one rung.

Two simple facts about semi-decidable sets serve as a starting point: they are exactly the projections of decidable relations ($x\in A\iff\exists y\,R(x,y)$), and the complement of a semi-decidable, non-decidable set is not semi-decidable. By alternating projections and complements, that is, quantifiers $\exists$ and $\forall$, we build more and more complicated sets. Sets are thus classified by the complexity of their logical description; Post’s theorem will show that this classification coincides with the one given by oracles.

Definition

Statements are classified by the number of alternations between “there exists” and “for all” needed to write them, starting from a simple check. A “there exists…” statement is at level 1 on the “there exists” side; a “for all…” statement at level 1 on the “for all” side; and so on.

Decidable sets are defined by a formula with no unbounded quantifier; semi-decidable sets, by a formula $\exists y\,R$. What happens with more quantifiers?

Let $n\ge1$. A set $A\subseteq\N$ is $\Sigma_n$ if there is a decidable predicate $R$ such that

$$x\in A\iff\exists y_1\,\forall y_2\,\exists y_3\cdots Q y_n\ R(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$$

($n$ alternating quantifiers starting with $\exists$). It is $\Pi_n$ if the formula starts with $\forall$. It is $\Delta_n$ if it is both $\Sigma_n$ and $\Pi_n$. We set $\Sigma_0=\Pi_0=\Delta_0$ = the decidable sets. A set is arithmetical if it belongs to one of these classes.

In plain words. A problem is at level $n$ if it can be stated with $n$ alternations of “there exists” and “for all” in front of a simple check. The levels on the “there exists” side and on the “for all” side are written $\Sigma_n$ and $\Pi_n$.

Each alternation adds a degree of ignorance. “There is a number that…” is confirmed by finding that number. “For every number…” is refuted by finding a counterexample, but is never confirmed by trial. “For every number, there is a number that…” is neither confirmed nor refuted by any finite trial. Goldbach’s conjecture is of the second type; the question “are there infinitely many twin primes?” is of the third.

Contraction of quantifiers. Two consecutive quantifiers of the same kind contract into one thanks to the pairing function: $\exists y\,\exists z\,R(x,y,z)\iff\exists w\,R(x,\pi_1w,\pi_2w)$, and likewise for $\forall$, the relation $R(x,\pi_1w,\pi_2w)$ remaining decidable. More generally, blocks of quantifiers of the same kind, $\exists y_1\cdots\exists y_k\,\forall z_1\cdots\forall z_l\cdots$, reduce to a strict alternation by coding each block by a single integer. Arbitrary blocks may therefore be allowed in the definition: only the number of alternations matters. One may also replace $x$ by a $k$-tuple, coded by an integer, which defines $\Sigma_n$ and $\Pi_n$ relations of any arity.

Arithmetical sets and formulas. Every formula of first-order arithmetic can be put in prenex form. Its quantifier-free part is decidable, since terms can be computed: every set defined by a formula of arithmetic is therefore arithmetical. Conversely, every decidable relation $R$ is defined by a $\Sigma_1$ formula, and so is its complement (representability, proved in the chapter Incompleteness without using anything from the present chapter). Replacing $R$ by this formula in the definition above, we see that every arithmetical set is definable. The arithmetical sets are thus exactly the sets definable in arithmetic, and the level $\Sigma_1$ corresponds to the $\Sigma_1$ formulas of the chapter Incompleteness. By the characterization of semi-decidable sets, $\Sigma_1$ is the class of semi-decidable sets; with the complement theorem and point 1 of the following lemma, $\Delta_1=\Delta_0$.

Relative computation, jump and one-one reductions

To compare impossible problems, we imagine machines equipped with oracles, and we say that a problem is “easier” than another if an oracle for the second makes it possible to solve the first. The “jump” of a problem is the halting problem for machines that have this problem as an oracle: it is always one step above.

We use machines equipped with an oracle (definition) and their equivalence with relative recursive functions (relative equivalence). We write $\varphi^A_e$ for the $e$-th $A$-computable function, $W^A_e$ for its domain, and we say that a set is semi-decidable in $A$ (or $A$-r.e.) if it is of the form $W^A_e$.

$A$ is Turing reducible to $B$, or decidable in $B$ ($A\le_T B$), if $c_A$ is $B$-recursive. If $A\le_TB$ and $B\le_TA$, $A$ and $B$ are Turing equivalent ($A\equiv_TB$); the equivalence class $\deg_T(A)$ is the Turing degree of $A$. For all $A,B,C$:

  1. (Relative normal form) $W^A_e=\{x:\exists y\ T^A_1(e,x,y)\}$, where $T^A_1$ is primitive recursive in $A$.
  2. (Projection) $X$ is semi-decidable in $A$ if and only if $X=\{x:\exists y\,R(x,y)\}$ for some relation $R$ decidable in $A$.
  3. (Complement) $X$ is decidable in $A$ if and only if $X$ and $\overline X$ are semi-decidable in $A$.
  4. (Transitivity) If $A\le_TB$ and $B\le_TC$, then $A\le_TC$; if $X$ is semi-decidable in $B$ and $B\le_TC$, then $X$ is semi-decidable in $C$. In particular $\le_T$ is a preorder, and $A\equiv_T\overline A$.

In plain words. A problem A reduces to a problem B if a program that could query an oracle for B would be able to solve A. Two problems that reduce to each other have the same “degree” of difficulty.

This is the most liberal way of comparing: we are allowed to ask the oracle as many questions as we like, and to adapt our questions to the previous answers.

$A\equiv_1B$ if and only if there is a computable permutation $h$ of $\N$ such that $h(A)=B$ (one says that $A$ and $B$ are recursively isomorphic, $A\equiv B$).

If $h$ exists, $h$ and $h^{-1}$ (computable: $h^{-1}(y)=\mu x\,[h(x)=y]$) are one-one reductions. Conversely, let $f:A\le_1B$ and $g:B\le_1A$. We build in stages an increasing sequence of finite bijections $p_s$, with $x\in A\iff p_s(x)\in B$ for all $x\in\mathrm{dom}\,p_s$, by a back-and-forth argument.

  • Forth (stage $2s+1$): let $x$ be the least integer outside $\mathrm{dom}\,p$. Compute $y=f(x)$; as long as $y\in\mathrm{ran}\,p$, replace $y$ by $f(p^{-1}(y))$. Add $(x,y)$ to $p$.
  • Back (stage $2s+2$): symmetrically, with the least $y$ outside $\mathrm{ran}\,p$, and $g$, $p$ in place of $f$, $p^{-1}$.

The loop terminates. The elements $x_1=p^{-1}(f(x)),\ x_2=p^{-1}(f(x_1)),\dots$ visited are in $\mathrm{dom}\,p$, which is finite. They are pairwise distinct: if $x_i=x_j$ with $i1$ this contradicts minimality, and if $i=1$ it gives $x=x_{j-1}\in\mathrm{dom}\,p$, a contradiction. The loop therefore stops after at most $|\mathrm{dom}\,p|$ rounds, on some $y\notin\mathrm{ran}\,p$.

Membership is preserved. $x\in A\iff f(x)\in B\iff x_1\in A$ (since $p$ preserves membership, applied to $x_1=p^{-1}(f(x))$) $\iff f(x_1)\in B\iff\cdots\iff y\in B$.

$h=\bigcup_sp_s$ is then a bijection (each $x$ enters the domain at a forth stage, each $y$ the range at a back stage), computable (run the stages until $x$ is in the domain), and $h(A)=B$.

Post’s theorem

Post’s theorem links the two ways of measuring difficulty: counting the alternations of quantifiers, and counting how many “jumps” above halting are needed to solve the problem. The two measures coincide.

We write $\emptyset'=K$ (since $\emptyset$ is decidable, $\varphi^\emptyset_e=\varphi_e$), $\emptyset''=K'$, and in general $\emptyset^{(n)}$ for the $n$-th jump of the empty set.

For all $n\ge0$:

  1. $B$ is $\Sigma_{n+1}$ if and only if $B$ is semi-decidable in $\emptyset^{(n)}$, if and only if $B$ is semi-decidable in some $\Sigma_n$ set, if and only if $B$ is semi-decidable in some $\Pi_n$ set.
  2. $\emptyset^{(n+1)}$ is $\Sigma_{n+1}$-complete.
  3. $B$ is $\Delta_{n+1}$ if and only if $B\le_T\emptyset^{(n)}$.

In plain words. A problem is at level $n+1$ on the “there exists” side exactly when it can be confirmed with the help of an oracle located $n$ jumps above the decidable.

Two measures of difficulty, one logical (counting the alternations), the other mechanical (counting the stacked oracles), give the same result. This kind of coincidence is the sign that a natural notion has been found.

Complete sets

Natural questions about programs can be placed precisely on the ladder. “Does this program halt on this input?” is on the first rung. “Does this program halt on all inputs?” is on the second. “Does this program compute a decidable set?” is on the third.

Bounding the level of a set from above is usually easy: write down its definition and apply the closure rules. Bounding it from below is harder: one shows that it is complete for a level, which, by the corollary, excludes it from the lower levels. The most natural examples are index sets $\{x:\varphi_x\in\mathcal C\}$, undecidable as soon as the class $\mathcal C$ is nontrivial (Rice’s theorem).

“Does this program always answer?” is strictly harder than “does this program answer on this input?”. It is the question a customer asks a software vendor: “does your program work in all cases?”. It is out of reach of every automatic tool, and even of every tool equipped with an oracle for halting.

Upper bounds. They are read off the formulas, with the closure rules ($y\in W_e$ is $\Sigma_1$, $y\notin W_e$ is $\Pi_1$):

  • $e\in\mathrm{Empty}\iff\forall y\,\forall s\,\neg T_1(e,y,s)$: $\Pi_1$;
  • $e\in\mathrm{Fin}\iff\exists n\,\forall y\,(y\le n\vee y\notin W_e)$: the parenthesis is $\Pi_1$, the whole $\Sigma_2$; $\mathrm{Inf}=\overline{\mathrm{Fin}}$ is $\Pi_2$;
  • $e\in\mathrm{Tot}\iff\forall y\,\exists s\,T_1(e,y,s)$: $\Pi_2$;
  • $e\in\mathrm{Cof}\iff\exists n\,\forall y\,(y\le n\vee y\in W_e)$: the parenthesis is $\Sigma_1$, hence $\Pi_2$ after $\forall y$, and the whole $\Sigma_3$;
  • $e\in\mathrm{Rec}\iff\exists i\,\forall y\,\big((y\in W_e\vee y\in W_i)\wedge\neg(y\in W_e\wedge y\in W_i)\big)$: $W_e$ is decidable if and only if its complement is some $W_i$; the parenthesis is the conjunction of a $\Sigma_1$ and a $\Pi_1$, hence $\Delta_2$, and the whole $\Sigma_3$;
  • $\mathrm{Comp}$: we always have $W_e\le_TK$ (a semi-decidable set reduces to $K$), so $e\in\mathrm{Comp}\iff K\le_TW_e\iff\exists j\,\forall z\,\exists v\,\big[\varphi^{W_e}_j(z)\conv=v\ \wedge\ ((v=1\wedge z\in K)\vee(v=0\wedge z\notin K))\big]$. By the use principle, “$\varphi^{W_e}_j(z)\conv=v$” can be written $\exists y\,\exists w\,[\dots\wedge w\text{ is the initial segment of }W_e]$, which is $\Sigma_2$ (fact (a) of the proof of Post’s theorem, with $C=W_e\in\Sigma_1$); the bracket is $\Sigma_2$, $\forall z\,\exists v$ gives $\Pi_3$, and $\exists j$ gives $\Sigma_4$.

Lower bounds. $K$: completeness (with the injective s-m-n, the reduction is one-one). $\mathrm{Empty}$: $\overline K\le_1\mathrm{Empty}$ via $x\mapsto f(x)$ with $\varphi_{f(x)}(z)\simeq\varphi_x(x)$; and every $\Pi_1$ set reduces to $\overline K$ (duality). $\mathrm{Fin}$, $\mathrm{Tot}$, $\mathrm{Inf}$: next proposition. $\{e:W_e\not\subseteq K\}$: weak jump. $\mathrm{Cof}$ and $\mathrm{Rec}$: proposition below. $\mathrm{Comp}$: the proposition after that, by an infinite-injury priority argument.

For every $A\in\Sigma_2$, there is a total computable injective $f$ such that $x\in A\Rightarrow W_{f(x)}$ is finite and $x\notin A\Rightarrow W_{f(x)}=\N$. Consequently $A\le_1\mathrm{Fin}$, and $\overline A\le_1\mathrm{Tot}$, $\overline A\le_1\mathrm{Inf}$ via the same function: $\mathrm{Fin}$ is $\Sigma_2$-complete, $\mathrm{Tot}$ and $\mathrm{Inf}$ are $\Pi_2$-complete.

In plain words. “Does this program halt on only finitely many inputs?” and “does it halt on all inputs?” are exactly on the second rung.

These two questions cannot be settled either by watching the programs, or even with a halting oracle: one would need an oracle for the halting of machines equipped with the halting oracle.

Let $x\in A\iff\exists y\,\forall z\,R(x,y,z)$ with $R$ decidable. The relation $Q(x,u)\iff\forall y\le u\ \exists z\ \neg R(x,y,z)$ is $\Sigma_1$ (bounded quantification), so the function $\psi(x,u)=0$ if $Q(x,u)$, undefined otherwise, is computable. By the injective s-m-n, there is an injective $f$ with $\varphi_{f(x)}(u)\simeq\psi(x,u)$, that is, $W_{f(x)}=\{u:Q(x,u)\}$.

  • If $x\in A$, let $y_0$ be such that $\forall z\,R(x,y_0,z)$. For $u\ge y_0$, $Q(x,u)$ is false (it fails at $y=y_0$), so $W_{f(x)}\subseteq\{0,\dots,y_0-1\}$ is finite.

Every $\Pi_2$ set is of the form $\overline A$ with $A\in\Sigma_2$; with the upper bounds above, this gives the three completeness results.

The program with index $f(x)$, written as an explicit search (it runs through the pairs $\langle y,z\rangle$ and stops only when every $y\le u$ has received a counter-witness $z$):

def phi_f(x):
    def prog(u):
        remaining = set(range(u + 1))     # the y ≤ u without a counter-witness
        z = 0
        while remaining:                  # infinite loop if some y never gets one
            remaining = {y for y in remaining if R(x, y, z)}
            z += 1
        return 0
    return prog

This program runs through the $z$ in order and removes $y$ as soon as some $z$ satisfies $\neg R(x,y,z)$; it halts if and only if $Q(x,u)$.

With the upper bounds above, $\mathrm{Cof}$ and $\mathrm{Rec}$ are $\Sigma_3$-complete (for $\le_1$), and their complements $\Pi_3$-complete.

For $A\subseteq\N$, let $H_A=\{x:W_x\cap A\ne\emptyset\}$. If $A\in\Pi_n$ ($n\ge1$), then $H_A\in\Sigma_{n+1}$; if $A$ is $\Pi_n$-complete, $H_A$ is $\Sigma_{n+1}$-complete. For example $H_{\overline K}=\{x:W_x\not\subseteq K\}$ is $\Sigma_2$-complete, and by iterating ($A\mapsto H_{\overline{H_A}}$…) one obtains a complete set at each level.

Upper bound. $x\in H_A\iff\exists y\,(y\in W_x\wedge y\in A)$: the parenthesis is the intersection of a $\Sigma_1$ and a $\Pi_n$, both $\Sigma_{n+1}$ by cumulativity, and the whole is $\Sigma_{n+1}$.

Completeness. Let $C\in\Sigma_{n+1}$: $x\in C\iff\exists y\,R(x,y)$ with $R\in\Pi_n$, that is, $\{\langle x,y\rangle:R(x,y)\}\in\Pi_n$. Since $A$ is $\Pi_n$-complete, there is a computable $f$ with $R(x,y)\iff f(\langle x,y\rangle)\in A$. By the injective s-m-n, there is an injective $g$ with $\varphi_{g(x)}(z)\simeq\mu y\,[f(\langle x,y\rangle)=z]$, so $W_{g(x)}=\{f(\langle x,y\rangle):y\in\N\}$. Then

$$x\in C\iff\exists y\ f(\langle x,y\rangle)\in A\iff W_{g(x)}\cap A\ne\emptyset\iff g(x)\in H_A.$$

Example: $\overline K$ is $\Pi_1$-complete (dual of the completeness of $K$), so $H_{\overline K}$ is $\Sigma_2$-complete. The complement of a $\Sigma_{n+1}$-complete set is $\Pi_{n+1}$-complete, which allows iteration.

A set $A$ is $\Delta_2$ if and only if its characteristic function is the limit of a computable sequence of functions: there is a total computable $g$ such that, for all $x$, $\chi_A(x)=\lim_{s\to\infty}g(x,s)$ (the sequence $g(x,0),g(x,1),\dots$ changes value only finitely often).

In plain words. The problems of level $\Delta_2$ are exactly those that can be “learned in the limit”: a program proposes a sequence of answers, corrects itself finitely many times, and ends up giving the right answer forever, without ever knowing that it has finished correcting itself.

This is a good image of experimental science: we put forward hypotheses, revise them in the light of new observations, and may end up being right without being able to be certain that we will never change our minds again.

If $A\le_T K$ via a machine $M$ (Post (3)), let $g(x,s)$ be the result of $M$ on $x$ when the oracle $K$ is replaced by its approximation $K_s=\{e:\varphi_e(e)\text{ halts in fewer than }s\text{ steps}\}$ (and 0 if this computation has not finished within $s$ steps). The actual computation queries only finitely many values of $K$, and the $K_s$ are eventually correct on these values, so $g(x,s)$ stabilizes on $\chi_A(x)$. Conversely, if $\chi_A=\lim_s g$, then $x\in A\iff\exists s\,\forall t>s\ g(x,t)=1$ ($\Sigma_2$) and likewise $x\notin A$ is $\Sigma_2$, so $A\in\Delta_2$.

Beyond

The ladder does not stop at statements about numbers: by speaking of infinite sets of numbers, we obtain even harder problems. And similar ladders exist in complexity, to measure no longer the impossible, but the very costly.

The whole hierarchy can be relativized: starting from the sets decidable in $A$ instead of the decidable sets, we obtain classes $\Sigma^A_n$, $\Pi^A_n$, and Post’s theorem holds with $A^{(n)}$ in place of $\emptyset^{(n)}$, with the same proofs. The analytical hierarchy also allows quantifiers over sets of integers (second-order logic); all arithmetical sets are at its first level, and not conversely (proposition below). At the other end, the polynomial hierarchy of complexity theory ($\mathsf P$, $\mathsf{NP}$, $\mathsf{coNP}$, $\Sigma^p_2$…) follows exactly the same pattern, with polynomially bounded quantifiers and polynomial-time oracles, to classify decidable sets according to the time needed to decide them (Three approaches to computation). The analogue of Post’s theorem is proved there below.

A set $A\subseteq\N$ is $\Sigma^1_1$ if there is an arithmetical relation $R$ (defined by a first-order formula containing a set variable) such that $x\in A\iff\exists X\subseteq\N\ R(x,X)$. It is $\Pi^1_1$ if $\forall X$ appears instead of $\exists X$, and $\Delta^1_1$ if it is both. Then every arithmetical set is $\Delta^1_1$, and the set

$$\emptyset^{(\omega)}=\{\langle n,x\rangle:x\in\emptyset^{(n)}\}$$

is $\Delta^1_1$ without being arithmetical.

If $A$ is arithmetical, $x\in A\iff\exists X\,(x\in A)\iff\forall X\,(x\in A)$, with a dummy variable $X$: $A$ is $\Delta^1_1$.

$\emptyset^{(\omega)}$ is $\Delta^1_1$. A set $Y$ codes the sequence of its slices $Y_i=\{z:\langle i,z\rangle\in Y\}$. Let $J(Y,n)$ be the formula “$Y_0=\emptyset$, and for all $i $$\langle n,x\rangle\in\emptyset^{(\omega)}\iff\exists Y\,\big(J(Y,n)\wedge x\in Y_n\big)\iff\forall Y\,\big(J(Y,n)\Rightarrow x\in Y_n\big).$$

$\emptyset^{(\omega)}$ is not arithmetical. If it were $\Sigma_k$, the set $\emptyset^{(k+1)}=\{x:\langle k+1,x\rangle\in\emptyset^{(\omega)}\}$ would be too (closure under composition with a computable function), hence it would be $\Pi_{k+1}$, which contradicts the corollary $\emptyset^{(k+1)}\notin\Pi_{k+1}$.

The analytical hierarchy continues further: $\Sigma^1_n$ and $\Pi^1_n$, defined by $n$ alternations of set quantifiers, form a strict hierarchy (Kleene, 1955), and $\Delta^1_1$ is exactly the class of hyperarithmetical sets, obtained by iterating the jump along the recursive ordinals (theorem of Suslin and Kleene). These two results are quoted without proof.

We work on binary words. $\mathsf P$ is the class of languages decided in polynomial time by a deterministic Turing machine (definition), and $\mathsf{NP}^O$ the class of languages accepted in polynomial time by a nondeterministic machine equipped with the oracle $O$ (a query to the oracle counts as one step). A language $A$ is $\Sigma^p_k$ if there are a polynomial $p$ and a relation $R\in\mathsf P$ such that

$$x\in A\iff\exists y_1\,\forall y_2\cdots Q y_k\ R(x,y_1,\dots,y_k),$$

where each $y_i$ ranges over words of length at most $p(|x|)$. $\Pi^p_k$ is defined with $\forall$ first, and $\Sigma^p_0=\mathsf P$. Then $\Sigma^p_1=\mathsf{NP}$, and for all $k\ge0$,

$$\Sigma^p_{k+1}=\mathsf{NP}^{\Sigma^p_k},$$

where $\mathsf{NP}^{\mathcal C}$ is the union of the $\mathsf{NP}^O$ for $O\in\mathcal C$. This is the exact analogue of Post’s theorem.

Preliminaries. As for the arithmetical hierarchy, blocks of quantifiers of the same kind contract into one, by coding an $m$-tuple of words by a word of polynomial length. The classes $\Sigma^p_k$ and $\Pi^p_k$ are therefore closed under conjunction: interleave the blocks of the two formulas, which have the same shape. They are also closed under a quantifier $\forall i\le m$, with $m$ polynomial, placed in front of a $\Pi^p_k$ formula: it joins the first block. Finally $\Pi^p_{k-1}\subseteq\Pi^p_k$, by dummy quantifiers. The case $\Sigma^p_1=\mathsf{NP}$ is the characterization of nondeterministic machines by certificates: guess the sequence of choices, and check the computation in polynomial time. It is the equality below for $k=0$, since an oracle in $\mathsf P$ can be replaced by computing its answers.

$\Sigma^p_{k+1}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}^{\Sigma^p_k}$. Let $x\in A\iff\exists y\,B(x,y)$, with $|y|\le p(|x|)$ and $B\in\Pi^p_k$. The complement $\overline B$ is $\Sigma^p_k$. The machine guesses $y$, asks the oracle $\overline B$ whether $(x,y)\in\overline B$, and accepts if the answer is no.

$\mathsf{NP}^{\Sigma^p_k}\subseteq\Sigma^p_{k+1}$. Let $N$ be a nondeterministic machine with oracle $O\in\Sigma^p_k$, running in time $p$. We have $x\in L(N^O)$ if and only if there is a word $c$, of polynomial length, that describes the choices of $N$, the list of queries $q_1,\dots,q_m$ asked and the answers $b_1,\dots,b_m$, and such that:

  1. $c$ describes an accepting computation of $N$ on $x$, when the oracle answers are $b_1,\dots,b_m$ (checkable in polynomial time);
  2. for every $i$ such that $b_i=1$, $q_i\in O$;
  3. for every $i$ such that $b_i=0$, $q_i\notin O$.

Write $q\in O\iff\exists w\,C(q,w)$ with $C\in\Pi^p_{k-1}$. The witnesses $w_i$ of the queries with a positive answer are moved into the leading existential block. Condition 2 becomes $\forall i\le m\,(b_i=1\Rightarrow C(q_i,w_i))$, which is $\Pi^p_{k-1}$, and condition 3 is $\Pi^p_k$. All lengths are polynomial in $|x|$, since $|q_i|\le p(|x|)$. The conjunction of 1, 2 and 3 is therefore $\Pi^p_k$, and $L(N^O)$ is obtained by preceding it with $\exists(c,w_1,\dots,w_m)$: it is $\Sigma^p_{k+1}$.

What does not carry over. The proof that the arithmetical hierarchy is strict relies on a diagonalization that has no known analogue here. It is not known whether $\Sigma^p_1\ne\Sigma^p_2$, nor even whether $\mathsf P\ne\mathsf{NP}$. All that is known is that if $\Sigma^p_k=\Pi^p_k$ for some $k$, the whole hierarchy collapses to that level (a classical result, not proved here).

These are upper bounds; for (a), (b), (c) they are optimal ($\Sigma_1$-, $\Pi_1$- and $\Pi_2$-complete respectively).

Show that $\Sigma_n\cup\Pi_n\subseteq\Delta_{n+1}$.

Let $A\in\Sigma_n$, defined by $\exists y_1\forall y_2\cdots R(x,\vec y)$. Dummy quantifiers can be added: $x\in A\iff\forall z\,\exists y_1\forall y_2\cdots R(x,\vec y)$ ($\Pi_{n+1}$ form), and $x\in A\iff\exists y_1\forall y_2\cdots Q y_n\,\overline Q z\ R(x,\vec y)$ ($\Sigma_{n+1}$ form, the quantifier added last being of the kind opposite to $Q$). Hence $A\in\Sigma_{n+1}\cap\Pi_{n+1}=\Delta_{n+1}$. Same reasoning for $\Pi_n$.

Show that $\mathrm{Fin}=\{e:W_e\text{ finite}\}$ is $\Sigma_2$ and that $\mathrm{Cof}=\{e:W_e\text{ cofinite}\}$ is $\Sigma_3$.

$W_e$ is finite if there is a bound beyond which no computation halts: $e\in\mathrm{Fin}\iff\exists n\,\forall x\,\forall s\,[x>n\Rightarrow\neg T(e,x,s)]$, a $\Sigma_2$ form after contracting $\forall x\forall s$.

$W_e$ is cofinite if, beyond some bound, every $x$ is in $W_e$: $e\in\mathrm{Cof}\iff\exists n\,\forall x\,[x>n\Rightarrow\exists s\,T(e,x,s)]$. The part $\forall x\,\exists s$ is $\Pi_2$, preceded by an $\exists$: $\Sigma_3$. These bounds are optimal (Fin is $\Sigma_2$-complete, Cof is $\Sigma_3$-complete).

Let $D=\{2x:x\in K\}\cup\{2x+1:x\notin K\}$. Show that $D\in\Delta_2$, but that $D$ is neither $\Sigma_1$ nor $\Pi_1$.

$D\in\Delta_2$. $y\in D\iff\big(y\text{ even}\wedge\exists s\,T(y/2,y/2,s)\big)\vee\big(y\text{ odd}\wedge\forall s\,\neg T(\tfrac{y-1}2,\tfrac{y-1}2,s)\big)$. This is a disjunction of a $\Sigma_1$ statement and a $\Pi_1$ statement; by the previous exercise, both are $\Delta_2$, and $\Delta_2$ is closed under disjunction (closures). One can also say that $D\le_TK$, and apply Post’s theorem.

Not $\Sigma_1$. If $D$ were semi-decidable, $\overline K=\{x:2x+1\in D\}$ would be too (test whether $2x+1$ belongs to $D$): false. Not $\Pi_1$. Otherwise $\overline D$ would be semi-decidable, and so would $\overline K=\{x:2x\in\overline D\}$. Thus $\Delta_2$ strictly contains $\Sigma_1\cup\Pi_1$.
