Each path is an ordered list of steps. A step points to a chapter or to a specific result; it is not necessary to read the whole chapter before moving on to the next one. The recommended level is chosen with the selector at the top of the page (philosophical, technical, technological). The technical chapters end with solved exercises, rated from one to three stars.

Paths by audience

Discovery (two hours)

For whom: any curious person, no prerequisites. Recommended level: philosophical.

  1. Non-computable functions exist: a counting argument, before any definition.
  2. Turing machines (beginning): what a computation is, with the simulator.
  3. The halting problem: the first problem that no program solves.
  4. Busy beavers: tiny machines about which we cannot know everything.
  5. Tiling the plane: the undecidable outside computer science.
  6. The incompleteness theorem: what it changes for mathematics.

Undergraduate course (one semester)

For whom: undergraduate students in computer science or mathematics. Recommended level: technical.

  1. Chapters 3 to 6 in order: Turing machines, recursive functions, λ-calculus, equivalence of the models.
  2. The Church-Turing thesis, to know what has been proved and what is assumed.
  3. Universality, then s-m-n and recursion.
  4. Undecidability (reductions, Rice’s theorem) and semi-decidability.
  5. The Post correspondence problem and its consequences for grammars.
  6. Alongside, the one- and two-star exercises of each of these chapters.

Graduate course

For whom: the continuation of the previous path. Recommended level: technical.

  1. The arithmetical hierarchy: classifying undecidable problems among themselves.
  2. Incompleteness, up to the second theorem and the decision problem.
  3. The MRDP theorem: the most elementary arithmetic is universal.
  4. Logic and types, then decidable theories.
  5. Kolmogorov complexity and the number Ω.
  6. Time and space complexity, up to Cook and Levin and Baker, Gill and Solovay, then randomness, approximation and quantum computing.
  7. The three-star exercises.

For programmers

For whom: developers who want to know what the theory says about their job. Recommended level: technological.

  1. Three approaches to computation: imperative, functional, logical, and the cost of simulations.
  2. The λ-calculus, with the built-in reducer.
  3. A compiler to Turing machines.
  4. Quines and the recursion theorem: a program that knows its own code.
  5. Rice’s theorem: why no static analyser is perfect.
  6. Total languages and Curry-Howard: types as specifications, programs as proofs; then polymorphism (System F) and proof assistants.
  7. Presburger arithmetic via automata, the theory behind SMT solvers.
  8. SAT solvers and program verification.
  9. Probabilistic algorithms and the first quantum algorithms, with their simulations.

For mathematicians

For whom: readers comfortable with proofs, who want to see the undecidable in their own field. Recommended level: technical.

  1. Recursive functions: computation defined without a machine, by equations.
  2. Halting and reductions: the method of all the proofs that follow.
  3. Diophantine equations: Pell’s equation, MRDP, the prime-representing polynomial.
  4. Decidable theories: Presburger, Tarski, and what separates them from arithmetic.
  5. The undecidable in ordinary mathematics: matrices, tilings, zeros of functions, groups, manifolds.
  6. Goldbach and halting, then classifying problems: halting, totality, finiteness of a domain, each on its own level.
  7. Busy beavers and ZFC: specific integers that set theory does not determine.

Philosophy of science

For whom: readers interested in the significance of the results more than in their proofs. Recommended level: philosophical.

  1. Motivations: why define computation.
  2. The Church-Turing thesis, its physical version, then physics as a text, and the living and consciousness.
  3. Is a theory of everything possible?
  4. Incompleteness and its misinterpretations.
  5. Incompleteness according to Chaitin: a theory proves no more information than it contains.
  6. What computability says about AI, and what it does not.
  7. Leibniz and binary: the dream of a universal calculus, three centuries earlier.

Computation and artificial intelligence

For whom: readers coming from machine learning. Recommended level: philosophical, then technical.

  1. Rice’s theorem: what cannot be known about a program by reading it.
  2. Programming from a specification and controlling an AI.
  3. Kolmogorov complexity is not computable, then no predictor learns everything.
  4. Neural networks as models of computation.
  5. The physical price of exhaustive search and SAT solvers.
  6. Curry-Howard and checking AI-produced proofs in Lean.
  7. Löb’s theorem: an AI reasoning about itself.

Thematic threads

These paths follow one idea through the whole manual. They assume the first chapters are known (at least the discovery path).

The diagonal

A single argument, Cantor’s, proves most of the impossibility theorems in the manual. Here we see it change costume.

  1. Cantor: there are more reals than integers.
  2. No total language captures everything computable.
  3. No total universal function.
  4. Halting.
  5. The recursion theorem: the diagonal becomes a fixed point.
  6. Gödel’s diagonal lemma.
  7. A total language does not contain its own interpreter.
  8. Berry’s paradox and busy beavers.
  9. The arithmetical hierarchy is strict.
  10. Every written theory of the world is countable.

Encoding a computation

To show that a question is undecidable, one shows that it can describe the history of a computation. This path follows the space-time diagram of a Turing machine across fields.

  1. Instantaneous descriptions: a computation is a sequence of words.
  2. Gödel numbers of computations and Kleene normal form.
  3. Cook and Levin: the computation becomes a propositional formula.
  4. The decision problem and one binary relation suffices: the computation becomes a logical statement.
  5. Wang tiles: the computation becomes a tiling.
  6. The word problem and Post’s dominoes: the computation becomes word rewriting.
  7. Matrix products: the computation becomes linear algebra.
  8. Cellular automata and rule 110: the computation becomes a local rule, and the space-time diagram draws itself.
  9. FRACTRAN and generalized Collatz: the computation becomes an arithmetic iteration.
  10. MRDP and zeros of functions: the computation becomes an equation.

Logic and foundations

From Hilbert’s question (can mathematics be mechanized?) to its answers.

  1. Formulas and truth, for the notation.
  2. The completeness theorem: logical truth is semi-decidable.
  3. The decision problem: it is not decidable.
  4. The boundary in logic: propositional, monadic, one binary relation.
  5. The incompleteness theorems, then Rosser.
  6. Decidable theories: the cases where Hilbert’s dream comes true.
  7. Curry-Howard and consistency through computation.
  8. System F, the λ-cube and the calculus of constructions, up to Lean and Rocq.
  9. A theory knows only finitely many digits of Ω.

The decidable and its price

Knowing that a problem is decidable is only the beginning: one still has to be able to pay for the computation.

  1. Two decidable cases of Post’s problem and decidable problems on grammars.
  2. Propositional calculus: decidable and $\mathrm{co}\mathsf{NP}$-complete.
  3. The hierarchy theorems: there are decidable problems of arbitrary difficulty; and why diagonalization does not settle P versus NP.
  4. Games and polynomial space.
  5. Randomness (primality, polynomial identities) and approximation: getting around the difficulty, or not.
  6. Presburger (at least doubly exponential) and Tarski.
  7. The Ackermann function: computable, but beyond all primitive recursion.
  8. Landauer’s principle: the physical limit of an enumeration.
  9. Verifying a neural network: decidable, but hard.
  10. What quantum computing changes, and what it does not.

Computation and the physical world

  1. Gandy’s theorem: discrete machines with local causality do not go beyond Turing.
  2. Cellular automata: a toy physics, local and parallel, that computes everything (rule 110).
  3. The physical thesis and the extended thesis.
  4. Randomness and quantum computing do not go beyond Turing; Shor and Grover change the cost.
  5. What an exhaustive search costs, with Landauer.
  6. The known values of the busy beavers and a toy Ω: how far real computation can go.
  7. A machine with bounded memory is a finite automaton.
  8. The undecidable spectral gap: the undecidable in a model of physics.
  9. Physics is a text, the living is not a blueprint.

A history in dates

The results in the order in which they appeared.

  1. 1703: Leibniz publishes his binary arithmetic.
  2. 1891: Cantor’s diagonal argument.
  3. 1900: Hilbert poses his tenth problem.
  4. 1931: Gödel proves incompleteness.
  5. 1936: Church and Turing define computation and solve the decision problem; this is the birth of the Church-Turing thesis.
  6. 1946–1947: Post, with the dominoes, then Post and Markov, with the word problem, find the first “combinatorial” undecidable problems.
  7. 1955–1958: the word problem for groups (Novikov, Boone).
  8. 1956–1957: Friedberg and Muchnik solve Post’s problem on degrees.
  9. 1958: Gödel’s System T.
  10. 1961–1966: Wang and Berger study tilings; in 1962, Radó defines the busy beavers.
  11. 1965–1975: Kolmogorov, Solomonoff and Chaitin found algorithmic information theory.
  12. 1969–1980: Howard formulates the correspondence between proofs and programs.
  13. 1970: Matiyasevich completes the negative solution of the tenth problem.
  14. 1971: Cook defines NP-completeness; in 1975, Baker, Gill and Solovay show that oracles do not settle it.
  15. 1972: Girard introduces System F; in 1988, Coquand and Huet define the calculus of constructions.
  16. 1976–1980: the Miller-Rabin primality test.
  17. 1985: Deutsch defines the quantum Turing machine; in 1994, Shor factors in quantum polynomial time.
  18. 2002: primality is in P (Agrawal, Kayal, Saxena).

Practice

The exercises are placed at the end of each technical chapter. One star: direct application of the course. Two stars: an idea to find. Three stars: a complete argument, often a result from the literature. Solutions are hidden; click to open them.

  • Models of computation: Turing machines, recursive functions, λ-calculus, equivalence.
  • Fundamental theorems: universality, s-m-n and recursion.
  • Limits of computation: undecidability, semi-decidability, arithmetical hierarchy, incompleteness, Diophantine equations, logic and types, decidable theories, ordinary mathematics, Kolmogorov and Ω, languages.
  • Complexity and case studies: complexity, Ackermann, busy beavers, AI, other models.
