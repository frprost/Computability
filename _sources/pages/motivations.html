Why a theory of computation?

We have all known since primary school what a computation is: a sequence of operations carried out according to rules, without thinking, until a result is obtained. Computers do nothing else, at a prodigious speed. It is natural to ask whether there are questions that no computation can answer. To find out, we must first say precisely what a computation is; that is the subject of computability.

Even though mathematics may seem obscure, even magical, part of mathematical activity can be automated and requires neither imagination nor creativity. Counting boards, the abacus, the mechanical calculators of Schickard (1623), Pascal (1642) or Leibniz (1673) are successive refinements of objects whose purpose is to compute. The question then naturally arises: is there a limit to what can be computed automatically, and if so, what is it?

This question, which might seem narrow, led, through many detours, to the invention of the computer. The computer is a singular object: it has no natural equivalent, and it was not invented to solve a physical problem (like the car, which moves people from one place to another) but to answer questions of a logical and philosophical nature about truth and proof. The first computers were limited for technological reasons; their principle, however, has not changed since: a computer from 1950 and a phone of today compute exactly the same functions, only the speed differs. The computer is the human product that comes closest to being the embodiment of an idea.

The question of computability as we conceive it today is recent. At the International Congress in Paris, in 1900, David Hilbert proposed a list of 23 problems; the second asked for a proof of the consistency of arithmetic, and the tenth for a method to decide whether a Diophantine equation has solutions (see the chapter Diophantine equations). In 1928, Hilbert and Ackermann explicitly formulated the decision problem (Entscheidungsproblem): find a mechanical procedure that decides whether a logical statement is universally valid. To answer no to such a question, one first had to define precisely what a mechanical procedure is: this is what Alonzo Church and Alan Turing did in 1936.

The problem existed, in fact, before it was posed. The scientific method, which consists in observing, building a model and comparing its predictions with experiment, presupposes that the computations within the model can be carried out. Yet defining a function is not enough to know how to compute it, and, as Turing showed, some computations cannot be carried out by any machine, independently of any technological consideration.

Note finally that all scientific output can be seen as the result of a single piece of software: a text editor. Books, videos, recordings can be digitized, that is, turned into a sequence of 0s and 1s. Science thus finds itself embedded inside a part of itself, computer science. This remark is not unrelated to the proof of Gödel’s incompleteness theorems, which rest on this technique of internalizing mathematics within itself by means of arithmetization (see the chapter Incompleteness). It suggests that the limits of computability apply to any structured discourse, hence to all sciences.

The intuitive notion of algorithm

An algorithm is a recipe: a finite list of precise instructions, which can be followed without understanding anything, and which produces a result. The recipe for long division is one. The examples that follow show that this simple idea hides some traps.

Before studying what can be computed automatically, we must specify what is meant by an “unambiguous and effective method”. Without formalizing anything yet, four remarks already frame the future definition.

Symbol manipulation

A machine does nothing but manipulate symbols, which we interpret. Numbers are an abstraction that does not exist as such in the material world: we represent the number seventeen by the symbols 17, the Romans by XVII, a computer by 10001. There must therefore always be an encoding of the inputs and an interpretation of the results.

Encoding requires an alphabet, the set of available symbols, and since the model must be concrete, this alphabet is finite: 26 Latin letters, 128 ASCII codes, a few tens of thousands of Chinese characters. But in the end every text stored in a memory is only a sequence of just two symbols, as Leibniz had shown (see the appendix Leibniz and binary). Mathematically, one can therefore always reduce to the integers by means of an encoding, and restricting attention to functions from $\N^n$ to $\N$ does not arbitrarily narrow the field of the computable.

Ch. Boitet’s lecture notes sum up this situation in a diagram: the function $f$ we want to compute lives in the world of abstract objects; the machine, for its part, transforms words. Computing $f$ means finding a transformation of words $M$ such that encoding, transforming and then interpreting gives the same result as applying $f$:

$$\begin{array}{ccc} x & \xrightarrow{\quad f\quad} & f(x)\\ {\scriptstyle\text{encoding}}\downarrow & & \uparrow{\scriptstyle\text{interpretation}}\\ \overline{x} & \xrightarrow[\text{machine}]{\quad M\quad} & M(\overline{x}) \end{array}$$

The diagram “commutes”: the two paths from $x$ to $f(x)$ give the same result. The choice of encoding is not innocent: with a bad encoding (for example one that wrote the answer in advance into the code of $x$), everything would become “computable”. We therefore require the encoding and the interpretation to be themselves simple and fixed once and for all, independently of $f$.

Likewise, nothing must be “magic”: each step must follow clear rules that can be carried out mechanically. This is the notion of a program. Whatever model is chosen, one will have to explain how computations are done, by a text, which can itself be encoded by an integer. It is therefore possible to number the programs (for instance by length, then in alphabetical order): program number 0, number 1, etc. A fundamental idea on which this whole manual rests thus appears: programs and data are of the same nature, they are integers.

This remark, harmless in appearance, is crucial. In old calculating machines, program and data were physically distinct. In the Pascaline (1642), designed to help Pascal’s father with his tax computations in Upper Normandy, the data were entered by wheels, the results appeared in windows, and the “program” was realized by toothed wheels driving one another; the machine added and subtracted, and multiplications and divisions were obtained by repetition. To do something else, another machine was needed. A computer, on the contrary, does not change its physical structure when an application is loaded: data are downloaded, which are then interpreted as a program. This is what makes it special: it is a machine that can simulate all the others (see Universality).

Unbounded finiteness

Every computation must take a finite time: one cannot pass to the limit. Analysis establishes that $\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} (1/2)^i = 2$, but no machine performs an infinite sum in finite time. Likewise the working space (sheet of paper, memory, wheels) must be finite at every moment, as must the alphabet and the program.

The working space must moreover be discrete: a real number contains an infinite amount of information (the sequence of its digits is infinite), and a memory cell that could hold an arbitrary real would hold an infinite “oracle”.

These limits must not, however, exclude a definition on the grounds that it would be hard to implement today. The power of a late-1970s supercomputer now fits in a pocket. This is why no bound is fixed: the available memory is “as large as one wishes”, which is different from “infinite”. At each moment it is finite, but more can always be added.

Definable functions and computable functions

Historically, operations were introduced through the way of carrying them out: to speak of addition, one must know how to add. But this is true only up to a point: defining a function does not always give a way of computing it.

Let $d_1 d_2 d_3\dots$ be the decimal digits of $\pi = 3.14159\dots$ Define, for $x\ge 1$:

$$f_1(x)=\begin{cases}1 &\text{if the digits of }\pi\text{ contain a block of exactly } x \text{ consecutive 5s (with no 5 on the left or right)},\\ 0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}$$ $$f_2(x)=\begin{cases}1 &\text{if the digits of }\pi\text{ contain a block of at least } x \text{ consecutive 5s},\\ 0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

It is not known today whether $f_1$ is computable. On the other hand, $f_2$ is computable, although nobody knows how to compute it.

In plain words. We build two functions from the digits of the number π (3.14159…). For each whole number, their value is well fixed, once and for all. Yet, for one of them, we know that a very short program computes it, without knowing which one.

Take the question: “is there somewhere in the digits of π a run of at least ten 5s in a row?”. The answer is yes or no, even if nobody knows it. Some cases have been checked: a single 5 appears as early as the fourth digit, two 5s side by side appear around the hundred and thirtieth, three 5s around the hundred and seventy-seventh.

The reasoning about the second function is surprising. Either π contains runs of 5s as long as we like, and then the answer is always “yes”; or there is a maximal length, and then the answer is “yes” up to that length and “no” after it. In both cases, a program of a few lines gives the right answer. But we do not know which of these programs is the right one.

This is the difference between knowing that an answer exists and knowing the answer. A detective may be sure that the culprit is one of the three people in the room without knowing which. Computability is about the existence of a method, not about what we know today. And the example already shows that computing and proving are linked: to know which program is the right one, we would have to prove a theorem about π.

Only two cases are possible.

  1. The blocks of consecutive 5s in the digits of $\pi$ have unbounded length. Then for every $x$ there is a block of length $\ge x$ and $f_2$ is the constant function equal to 1.
  2. There is a maximal length $k$ (possibly $k=0$). Then $f_2(x)=1$ if $x\le k$ and $0$ otherwise.

In both cases $f_2$ is computed by a trivial program: “answer 1”, or “compare $x$ with the constant $k$”. The proof of computability is non-constructive: it uses the excluded middle (case 1 or case 2) without saying which one holds, nor, in the second case, the value of $k$.

For $f_1$, the argument fails: knowing that $\pi$ contains blocks of length 7 says nothing about the existence of a block of length exactly 6. We know that $f_1$ is semi-computable on the value 1 (the block is eventually found if it exists); its computability is open. It would follow, for instance, from the normality of $\pi$ (conjectured, not proved), which would make $f_1$ constantly equal to 1.

The two candidate programs for $f_2$, written in an ordinary language:

// case 1                      // case 2, for some unknown constant K
f2(x) = 1                      f2(x) = if x ≤ K then 1 else 0

And here is a program that semi-computes $f_1$: it halts with answer 1 if a block of exactly $x$ fives exists, and searches forever otherwise. It uses a generator of the digits of $\pi$ (the “spigot” algorithm of Rabinowitz and Wagon, which produces the digits one by one using integers only).

search(x) :
  block := 0
  for each digit d of π (in order) :
    if d = 5 then block := block + 1
    else
      if block = x then answer 1 and halt
      block := 0

The distinction between these two programs, one that always terminates, the other that may search forever, is the subject of the chapter Semi-decidability.

Conversely, not knowing how to define something does not always prevent one from realizing it: intelligence or consciousness are very hard to define precisely, and yet they are passed on and developed, through education, without anyone being able to write down their definition. The relation between defining and realizing is simple in neither direction.

Partiality is unavoidable

Some intuitively computable functions are partial: division is undefined when the divisor is zero. One might think of circumventing the problem as mathematicians often do, by adding to the codomain a special value “no result”, and considering only programs that always halt. A very general argument shows that this is impossible.

Consider a formalism of programs $P_0, P_1, P_2,\dots$ that can be effectively enumerated, each of which computes a total function $f_n:\N\to\N$, and such that $P_n$ can be run on any given input. Then the function $g(n)=f_n(n)+1$ is intuitively computable and total, but is computed by no $P_i$.

In plain words. Imagine a programming language in which every program always ends up halting. There is then necessarily a function that we know how to compute by hand but that no program of this language computes. To be able to compute everything, we must accept programs that sometimes run forever.

The reasoning resembles the liar paradox. Let us list all the programs of the language: the first, the second, the third… Let us build a new function as follows: for the number 1, we run the first program and add 1 to its answer; for the number 2, we run the second and add 1; and so on. This function is easy to compute, since every program halts. But it differs from the first program at 1, from the second at 2, from the third at 3: it appears nowhere in the list.

The lesson is surprising: a safe language, in which nothing can go round in circles, is necessarily incomplete. Everyday programming languages (Python, C, Java) accept the risk of an infinite loop so as to be able to compute everything. Some programs that check proofs, such as Rocq (formerly Coq), Agda or Lean, make the opposite choice: all their programs halt, and they give up certain functions.

A third answer then appears, beside “yes” and “no”: “no answer yet”. It is not symmetric. If a program halts, we end up seeing it; if it does not halt, we may wait forever without ever being sure. Justice knows this asymmetry: guilt must be proved, and without proof one remains presumed innocent. Not knowing that a thing is true is not the same as knowing that it is false. The whole rest of the manual, from the halting problem to semi-decidable sets, explores this difference.

$g$ is computable: to compute $g(n)$, enumerate the programs until $P_n$ is obtained (it is the $(n+1)$th in the list, since numbering starts at 0), run it on $n$ (it halts by hypothesis) and add 1. It is total.

Suppose $g=f_i$ for some index $i$, that is, $g(n)=f_i(n)$ for all $n$. At $n=i$: $f_i(i)=g(i)=f_i(i)+1$, a contradiction.

If, on the other hand, the formalism contains programs that do not terminate, the argument breaks down at the right place: $g(n)=f_n(n)+1$ is no longer necessarily defined, and for the index $i$ of a program computing $g$, the equation $f_i(i)\simeq f_i(i)+1$ simply says that $f_i(i)$ is undefined. No contradiction.

Concretely, take as total formalism the language of primitive recursive functions: each term is a program that terminates, and the terms can be enumerated and evaluated. The diagonal function $g(n)=f_n(n)+1$ is then computable by an ordinary program containing a “while” loop (the term evaluator), but by no primitive recursive term. The Ackermann function is an explicit example of the same phenomenon, obtained by a finer diagonalization.

The simulator below runs a machine that never halts on any input: it computes the nowhere-defined function, which a complete formalism must be able to express.

q0 B R q0

Non-computable functions exist

Whatever formalism of programs is adopted (finite alphabet, finite programs), there are functions from $\N$ to $\N$, and even from $\N$ to $\{0,1\}$, that are computed by no program.

In plain words. There are functions that no program, written in any language, can compute. The reason is simple: there are many more functions than programs.

A program is a finite text. All possible texts can be arranged in a list: first those of one letter, then those of two letters, and so on. Programs can therefore be numbered like the integers 1, 2, 3… On the other hand, the functions that assign 0 or 1 to each integer are infinite sequences of 0s and 1s, and Cantor showed that they cannot all be numbered (see the appendix on Cantor). So there must remain functions without a program.

It is a matter of numbers, not of technique: no progress in computers will close this gap, because it comes from what a finite description is. The reasoning proves that such functions exist without showing a single one. The chapter Undecidability will give a very concrete one: knowing whether a program will eventually halt.

A program is a finite word over a finite alphabet $\Sigma$. The set $\Sigma^*$ of words is countable: words are enumerated by increasing length, and among words of equal length in alphabetical order. The set of computable functions is therefore at most countable.

The set $\{0,1\}^{\N}$ of functions from $\N$ to $\{0,1\}$ is not countable (Cantor’s diagonal argument): if $(h_n)$ is a sequence of such functions, $h(n)=1-h_n(n)$ differs from each $h_n$ at $n$. Hence non-computable functions exist; “almost all” functions are non-computable, in the sense that the computable functions form a countable part of an uncountable set.

The enumeration of words by length and then alphabetical order is an explicit bijection between $\Sigma^*$ and $\N$. For the alphabet $\{a,b\}$:

rank01234567…
wordεabaaabbabbaaa…

For an alphabet with $k$ letters $a_1,\dots,a_k$, the rank of the word $a_{i_1}\cdots a_{i_m}$ is $\sum_{j=1}^m i_j\,k^{m-j}$ (“bijective” base-$k$ notation, without a zero digit). The chapter Encodings gives other encodings, better suited to proofs.
