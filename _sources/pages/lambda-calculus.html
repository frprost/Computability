In secondary school, we write $f(x)=x^2+1$ and compute $f(3)$ by replacing $x$ with 3. The λ-calculus keeps only that: defining a function, and applying it by replacing its parameter with a value. There are not even numbers to begin with; they are made out of functions.

The American mathematician Alonzo Church proposed this language in 1936, a few months before Turing, to answer the same question: what is a computation? The two answers, one made of mechanics and the other of pure substitution, turned out to be equivalent. The λ-calculus is today the heart of programming languages such as OCaml and Haskell, and of all the “anonymous functions” of Python or JavaScript.

Variable, abstraction, application

We write $\lambda x.\,x+1$ for “the function that maps $x$ to $x+1$”, without giving it a name. The Greek letter λ (lambda) simply signals that a function is being defined. Applying this function to the number 3 means writing the two side by side, then replacing $x$ by 3 in the body. The whole language comes down to these three gestures: naming a variable, making a function, applying a function to something.

In the 1930s, Alonzo Church was looking for a general theory of functions, initially as a foundation for logic. The λ-calculus that came out of it is one of the first models of computation to give a precise definition of computable functions (λ-definability, 1933–1936), contemporary with the general recursive functions of Herbrand and Gödel (1934) and with Turing machines (1936).

The idea of a function goes back to Leibniz, who introduced the word at the end of the seventeenth century; Johann Bernoulli and then Euler redefined it as an expression made up of variable and constant quantities. The expression $x+1$ has a value only once that of $x$ is fixed; the function $f:x\mapsto x+1$ expresses this dependence, and computing $f(4)$ consists in substituting $4$ for $x$ in $x+1$. Three concepts are at work:

  • Variable: a symbol that stands for “any value”.
  • Abstraction: the variable is only a means of indicating what is to be replaced; $y\mapsto y+1$ and $x\mapsto x+1$ are the same function (dummy variable).
  • Application: evaluating a function means replacing the variable by a value.

Church’s stroke of genius was to use only these three principles, and to accept that any term, including a function, can be substituted for a variable. Usual mathematical notation confuses the function $f$ with its value $f(x)$; it forces us to name functions, which makes higher order (functions that take or return functions, like a compiler) and partial application awkward: if $g(x,y)=x+y+1$, the function “$g$ with $x=4$” is written simply $\lambda y.\,g\,4\,y$.

Syntax and computation rule

The only computation rule is the replacement of the parameter by the argument, exactly as when computing $f(3)$. One must only take care not to confuse two variables that bear the same name, just as in English “she” can designate two different people in two neighbouring sentences.

Let $\mathcal V$ be a countably infinite set of variables. The λ-terms are defined by the grammar

$$M ::= x \;\mid\; (M_1\,M_2) \;\mid\; \lambda x.M \qquad (x\in\mathcal V).$$

$(M_1\,M_2)$ is an application, $\lambda x.M$ an abstraction. The free variables $\mathrm{FV}$ and bound variables $\mathrm{BV}$ are defined by

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{FV}(x)&=\{x\} & \mathrm{FV}(M_1M_2)&=\mathrm{FV}(M_1)\cup\mathrm{FV}(M_2) & \mathrm{FV}(\lambda x.M)&=\mathrm{FV}(M)\setminus\{x\}\\ \mathrm{BV}(x)&=\emptyset & \mathrm{BV}(M_1M_2)&=\mathrm{BV}(M_1)\cup\mathrm{BV}(M_2) & \mathrm{BV}(\lambda x.M)&=\mathrm{BV}(M)\cup\{x\} \end{aligned}$$

In plain words. An expression of the λ-calculus is built with three rules: a variable ($x$) is an expression; “the function that maps $x$ to $M$”, written $\lambda x.M$, is an expression; “$M$ applied to $N$”, written $M\,N$, is an expression.

A variable is “bound” when it is the parameter of a function that surrounds it, like $x$ in $f(x)=x+1$: its name does not matter, it could be called $t$. It is “free” otherwise, like $a$ in $x+a$, whose value must come from elsewhere. This distinction, commonplace in secondary school, demands precision as soon as we want a machine to compute.

Notational conventions: application associates to the left ($MNP$ abbreviates $((MN)P)$), the body of an abstraction extends as far to the right as possible, and $\lambda xy.M$ abbreviates $\lambda x.\lambda y.M$.

In the reducer on this page, write λx.M or \x.M, λx y.M for $\lambda x.\lambda y.M$; the integers 0, 1, 2… denote the Church numerals defined below.

Substitution $M[x:=N]$ (replacing the free occurrences of $x$ by $N$) is defined by induction:

$$\begin{aligned} x[x:=N]&=N, \qquad y[x:=N]=y\ \ (y\neq x),\qquad (M_1M_2)[x:=N]=(M_1[x:=N]\ M_2[x:=N]),\\ (\lambda x.M)[x:=N]&=\lambda x.M,\\ (\lambda y.M)[x:=N]&=\lambda y.\,M[x:=N] \quad\text{if } y\ne x \text{ and } y\notin\mathrm{FV}(N),\\ (\lambda y.M)[x:=N]&=\lambda z.\,M[y:=z][x:=N] \quad\text{if } y\ne x,\ y\in\mathrm{FV}(N), \text{ with } z \text{ fresh.} \end{aligned}$$

Two terms are α-equivalent if they differ only in the names of bound variables: $\lambda x.M=_\alpha\lambda z.M[x:=z]$ for $z\notin\mathrm{FV}(M)\cup \mathrm{BV}(M)$, and this relation is closed under passing to subterms. From now on we work modulo $=_\alpha$.

β-reduction $\to_\beta$ is the smallest relation such that $(\lambda x.M)\,N\to_\beta M[x:=N]$ and closed under context: if $M\to_\beta M'$, then $MN\to_\beta M'N$, $NM\to_\beta NM'$ and $\lambda x.M\to_\beta\lambda x.M'$. We write $\twoheadrightarrow_\beta$ for its reflexive and transitive closure, and $=_\beta$ for the equivalence relation it generates.

In plain words. To compute “the function $\lambda x.M$ applied to $N$”, we replace $x$ by $N$ in $M$. It is the only computation rule of the language. A parameter can also be renamed without changing the meaning at all.

This is the gesture made at school to compute $f(4)$. The only subtlety is to avoid name collisions: if $N$ contains a variable with the same name as a bound variable of $M$, the latter is renamed first, so as not to mix two distinct things.

A subterm of the form $(\lambda x.M)N$ is called a redex. A term without a redex is a normal form. A term has a normal form if it reduces to a normal form.

The reducer avoids capture problems by representing bound variables with de Bruijn indices (the number of the λ that binds them, counted from the inside out), and goes back to names only for display, adding primes when needed.

A first computation

Take a function of two arguments $a$ and $b$ that applies $a$ to $b$, applied to “iterate twice” and then to “iterate three times”:

$$t=(\lambda a.\lambda b.\,a\,b)\ (\lambda f.\lambda x.\,f(f\,x))\ (\lambda g.\lambda y.\,g(g(g\,y))).$$

Write $\mathbf 2=\lambda f.\lambda x.f(fx)$ and $\mathbf 3=\lambda g.\lambda y.g(g(gy))$. Two reductions give $t\twoheadrightarrow_\beta \mathbf2\,\mathbf3$, then:

$$\mathbf2\,\mathbf3\to_\beta\lambda x.\,\mathbf3(\mathbf3\,x)\twoheadrightarrow_\beta\lambda x.\lambda y.\,x(x(x(x(x(x(x(x(x\,y)))))))) = \mathbf 9.$$

Iterating “iterate three times” twice means iterating $3^2=9$ times. 

(λa.λb.(a b)) (λf.λx.(f (f x))) (λg.λy.(g (g (g y))))

The computation may also never end. With $\delta=\lambda x.x\,x$, the term $\delta\,\delta=(\lambda x.x\,x)(\lambda x.x\,x)$ reduces to itself: it is the equivalent of an infinite loop. It rests on self-application $x\,x$, which makes no sense for a function in the set-theoretic sense, but does for a program: a program can receive its own text as input. It is this reflexivity that gives the λ-calculus its power, and makes it less intuitive than Turing machines.

Confluence

In a complicated expression, we can often choose where to start the computation. Can these choices lead to different results? No: this is the first great theorem of the λ-calculus. On the other hand, some choices may lead to an endless computation where others succeed. Fortunately, there is a strategy that always succeeds when success is possible.

If $M\twoheadrightarrow_\beta N_1$ and $M\twoheadrightarrow_\beta N_2$, there is $Q$ such that $N_1\twoheadrightarrow_\beta Q$ and $N_2\twoheadrightarrow_\beta Q$. In particular, a term has at most one normal form.

In plain words. Whatever the order in which the replacements are made, one cannot end up with two different final results.

As at school: to compute $(2+3)\times(4+1)$, we can start from the left or from the right, we always find 25. What is obvious for arithmetic requires a real proof here, because some orders of computation can run forever. The theorem at least guarantees that the result, if it exists, is unique.

We follow the method of Tait and Martin-Löf. Define parallel reduction $\Rightarrow$:

$$\frac{}{x\Rightarrow x}\qquad\frac{M\Rightarrow M'}{\lambda x.M\Rightarrow\lambda x.M'}\qquad\frac{M\Rightarrow M'\quad N\Rightarrow N'}{MN\Rightarrow M'N'}\qquad\frac{M\Rightarrow M'\quad N\Rightarrow N'}{(\lambda x.M)N\Rightarrow M'[x:=N']}$$

It reduces simultaneously any set of redexes present in the term. We have $\to_\beta\subseteq\Rightarrow\subseteq\twoheadrightarrow_\beta$, so the transitive closure of $\Rightarrow$ is $\twoheadrightarrow_\beta$.

Substitution lemma. If $M\Rightarrow M'$ and $N\Rightarrow N'$ then $M[x:=N]\Rightarrow M'[x:=N']$ (induction on the derivation of $M\Rightarrow M'$, using $M[y:=P][x:=N]=M[x:=N][y:=P[x:=N]]$ for $y\notin\mathrm{FV}(N)$).

Complete development. Let $M^*$ be the term obtained by reducing all the redexes of $M$: $x^*=x$, $(\lambda x.M)^*=\lambda x.M^*$, $((\lambda x.M)N)^*=M^*[x:=N^*]$, and $(MN)^*=M^*N^*$ if $M$ is not an abstraction. By induction on $M\Rightarrow M'$ and thanks to the substitution lemma: if $M\Rightarrow M'$ then $M'\Rightarrow M^*$.

Thus $\Rightarrow$ has the diamond property: if $M\Rightarrow N_1$ and $M\Rightarrow N_2$, then $N_1\Rightarrow M^*$ and $N_2\Rightarrow M^*$. A relation with the diamond property passes it on to its transitive closure (tile the rectangle formed by two sequences of steps, square by square). Since this closure is $\twoheadrightarrow_\beta$, confluence follows. Uniqueness of the normal form: if $N_1,N_2$ are normal and $M\twoheadrightarrow N_1$, $M\twoheadrightarrow N_2$, the common $Q$ satisfies $N_1\twoheadrightarrow Q$, so $Q=N_1$, and likewise $Q=N_2$.

The diamond property fails for $\to_\beta$ itself: reducing one redex can duplicate another redex, which must then be reduced several times. This is why we go through $\Rightarrow$.

There is also another classical proof, Barendregt’s (The Lambda Calculus, ch. 11), using an “underlined” λ-calculus in which the redexes to be followed are marked, a function $\varphi$ that reduces all underlined redexes in parallel, an erasure function $|\cdot|$ and three commutation lemmas leading to the “strip lemma”. Both proofs rest on the same idea (reducing a set of redexes as a block); that of Tait and Martin-Löf avoids underlined terms.

An example where $\to_\beta$ does not have the diamond property: $M=(\lambda x.x\,x)((\lambda y.y)z)$. In one step, $M\to(\lambda y.y)z\,((\lambda y.y)z)$ or $M\to(\lambda x.xx)z$. The first term needs two steps to reach $z\,z$, the second only one.

Weak head reduction $\to_t$ contracts the leftmost redex when it is not under an abstraction:

$$(\lambda y.P)\,Q\,M_1\cdots M_n\ \to_t\ P[y:=Q]\,M_1\cdots M_n\qquad(n\ge0),$$

and nothing else (it never reduces under a $\lambda$). It is deterministic. A term with no such redex is a weak head normal form: it is either an abstraction $\lambda x.P$ or a term $v\,M_1\cdots M_n$ whose head is a variable.

Theorem. If $M\twoheadrightarrow_\beta N$ and $N$ is a weak head normal form, then the weak head reduction of $M$ terminates. In particular, if a closed term has a normal form, its weak head reduction terminates (a closed normal form is an abstraction).

In plain words. To know whether a computation can succeed, it is enough to follow the laziest strategy, which computes only what is immediately needed.

This is the strategy of working “on demand”: never compute something that is not yet needed. It is used by languages such as Haskell, and it even makes it possible to handle infinite lists of which only the beginning is computed.

We use parallel reduction $\Rightarrow$ and its substitution lemma, proved for confluence. A step $M\Rightarrow N$ is internal, written $M\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}N$, if it does not contract the weak head redex, that is, if we are in one of three cases: (i) $M=\lambda x.P$, $N=\lambda x.P'$ with $P\Rightarrow P'$; (ii) $M=v\,M_1\cdots M_n$, $N=v\,N_1\cdots N_n$ with $M_i\Rightarrow N_i$; (iii) $M=(\lambda y.P)\,Q\,M_1\cdots M_n$, $N=(\lambda y.P')\,Q'\,N_1\cdots N_n$ with $P\Rightarrow P'$, $Q\Rightarrow Q'$, $M_i\Rightarrow N_i$.

Fact 0. If $M\to_tM'$, then $M[x:=Q]\to_tM'[x:=Q]$ and $M\,R\to_tM'\,R$ (the weak head redex stays at the head). If $M\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}M'$ and $R\Rightarrow R'$, then $M\,R\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}M'\,R'$ (in case (i), $(\lambda x.P)\,R\Rightarrow(\lambda x.P')\,R'$ is of type (iii)). If $M\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}M'$ and $Q\Rightarrow Q'$ and the head of $M$ is not $x$, then $M[x:=Q]\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}M'[x:=Q']$ (substitution lemma, component by component).

Lemma 1. If $M\Rightarrow N$, there is $L$ such that $M\to_t^*L\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}N$. Write $M\rhd N$ for this property. By induction on the derivation of $M\Rightarrow N$.

  • $x\Rightarrow x$ and $\lambda x.P\Rightarrow\lambda x.P'$ are internal: $L=M$.
  • $P\,Q\Rightarrow P'\,Q'$ without contracting the root. By the induction hypothesis, $P\to_t^*L\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}P'$, so, by Fact 0, $P\,Q\to_t^*L\,Q\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}P'\,Q'$.
  • $(\lambda y.P)\,Q\Rightarrow P'[y:=Q']$ with $P\Rightarrow P'$ and $Q\Rightarrow Q'$. By the induction hypothesis, $P\to_t^*L\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}P'$ and $Q\rhd Q'$. First $(\lambda y.P)\,Q\to_tP[y:=Q]\to_t^*L[y:=Q]$ (Fact 0). If the head of $L$ is not $y$, Fact 0 gives $L[y:=Q]\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}P'[y:=Q']$. Otherwise $L=y\,L_1\cdots L_n$ and $P'=y\,P_1\cdots P_n$ with $L_i\Rightarrow P_i$; then $L[y:=Q]=Q\,L_1^\ast\cdots L_n^\ast$ and $P'[y:=Q']=Q'\,P_1^\ast\cdots P_n^\ast$, where $L_i^\ast=L_i[y:=Q]\Rightarrow P_i^\ast=P_i[y:=Q']$. Since $Q\to_t^*G\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}Q'$, Fact 0 (applied $n$ times) gives $Q\,\vec L^\ast\to_t^*G\,\vec L^\ast\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}Q'\,\vec P^\ast$.

Lemma 2. If $M\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}N\to_tN'$, then $M\to_tM''\Rightarrow N'$ for some $M''$. Indeed $N$ has a weak head redex, so the internal step is of type (iii): $M=(\lambda y.P)\,Q\,\vec M$, $N=(\lambda y.P')\,Q'\,\vec N$, $N'=P'[y:=Q']\,\vec N$. Take $M''=P[y:=Q]\,\vec M$; then $M''\Rightarrow N'$ by the substitution lemma.

Proof of the theorem. Since $\to_\beta\subseteq\Rightarrow$, we have $M=M_0\Rightarrow M_1\Rightarrow\cdots\Rightarrow M_k=N$. We show, by induction on $k$, that $M\to_t^*L$ for a weak head normal form $L$. For $k=0$, $L=N$. Otherwise, by the induction hypothesis, $M_1\to_t^mL_1$ with $L_1$ a weak head normal form. By Lemma 1, $M\to_t^*G_0\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}M_1$. If $M_1\to_tM_1'$, Lemma 2 gives $G_0\to_tK'\Rightarrow M_1'$, then Lemma 1 gives $G'\to_t^*G_1\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}M_1'$. Repeating $m$ times, we get $M\to_t^*G_m\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{int}}L_1$. If $G_m$ had a weak head redex, the internal step would be of type (iii) and $L_1$ would have one too; so $G_m$ is a weak head normal form. Since weak head reduction is deterministic, that of $M$ terminates on $G_m$.

Finally, if $M$ is closed and has a normal form $N$, then $N$ is closed; a closed normal term is not of the form $v\,\vec M$ (the variable $v$ would be free), so it is an abstraction, and the theorem applies.

Two useful remarks. The reducts of an abstraction $\lambda x.P$ are abstractions $\lambda x.P'$ with $P\twoheadrightarrow P'$; the reducts of a term $v\,M_1\cdots M_n$ are terms $v\,M'_1\cdots M'_n$ (no redex can appear at the head). With confluence, we deduce: if $M\twoheadrightarrow_tL$ with $L$ a weak head normal form and $M\twoheadrightarrow\lambda x.N_0$ with $\lambda x.N_0$ normal, then $L=\lambda x.L_0$ with $L_0\twoheadrightarrow N_0$; if $M\twoheadrightarrow v\,N_1\cdots N_n$ normal, then $L=v\,L_1\cdots L_n$ with $L_i\twoheadrightarrow N_i$.

If $M$ has a normal form, the strategy that always reduces the leftmost outermost redex (normal order) reaches it.

In plain words. There is a computation strategy, always the same, that finds the final result whenever there is one: always perform the leftmost, outermost replacement.

Other strategies can get lost in an endless computation, for example by insisting on computing an argument that will never be used. The normal strategy avoids this trap by evaluating an argument only when it is really used. The reducer of the page and the Python program of the manual apply it.

By induction on the size of the normal form $N$ of $M$. By the weak head normalization theorem, the weak head reduction of $M$ terminates on a weak head normal form $L$. As long as a term has a weak head redex, this redex is the leftmost outermost one: normal order therefore begins with these steps, and arrives at $L$. By the second remark following that theorem, there are two cases.

  • $N=\lambda x.N_0$ and $L=\lambda x.L_0$ with $L_0\twoheadrightarrow N_0$. The leftmost outermost redex of $\lambda x.L_0$ is that of $L_0$: normal order continues with that of $L_0$, which reaches $N_0$ by the induction hypothesis ($N_0$ is smaller than $N$).
  • $N=v\,N_1\cdots N_n$ and $L=v\,L_1\cdots L_n$ with $L_i\twoheadrightarrow N_i$. The leftmost outermost redex of $L$ lies in the first $L_i$ that is not normal. Normal order therefore reduces $L_1$ according to its own normal order, which reaches $N_1$ by the induction hypothesis, then $L_2$, and so on up to $L_n$.

In both cases, normal order reaches $N$. This is recovered below as a consequence of Curry and Feys’s standardization theorem.

If $M\twoheadrightarrow_\beta N$, there is a standard reduction from $M$ to $N$: first reduce at the weak head, then treat the subterms from left to right, never returning to a subterm already left. Formally, the relation $M\twoheadrightarrow_sN$ is defined by:

  • (S1) if $M\to_t^*x$ (a variable), then $M\twoheadrightarrow_sx$;
  • (S2) if $M\to_t^*\lambda x.P$ and $P\twoheadrightarrow_sP'$, then $M\twoheadrightarrow_s\lambda x.P'$;
  • (S3) if $M\to_t^*P\,Q$, $P\twoheadrightarrow_sP'$ and $Q\twoheadrightarrow_sQ'$, then $M\twoheadrightarrow_sP'\,Q'$.

The theorem says: $M\twoheadrightarrow_\beta N$ implies $M\twoheadrightarrow_sN$.

In plain words. Every computation, even one carried out in disorder, can be reorganized so as to proceed “from left to right and from the outside in”.

So a computation can always be put back into a standard order without changing what it produces. It is a technical result used to prove the previous ones.

The inductive form above is due to Takahashi (1995). Each derivation of $M\twoheadrightarrow_sN$ describes a sequence of reductions: the weak head steps, then (S2) those of $P$ under the $\lambda$, or (S3) those of $P$ followed by those of $Q$. In particular $M\twoheadrightarrow_sN$ implies $M\twoheadrightarrow_\beta N$. We use Fact 0 of weak head normalization: if $M\to_tM'$, then $M[x:=Q]\to_tM'[x:=Q]$ and $M\,R\to_tM'\,R$.

Lemma 1. $M\twoheadrightarrow_sM$, by induction on $M$, with zero head steps.

Lemma 2. If $M\to_t^*M'$ and $M'\twoheadrightarrow_sN$, then $M\twoheadrightarrow_sN$: add the head steps in front of those of the rule used.

Lemma 3 (substitution). If $M\twoheadrightarrow_sM'$ and $L\twoheadrightarrow_sL'$, then $M[x:=L]\twoheadrightarrow_sM'[x:=L']$. By induction on the derivation of $M\twoheadrightarrow_sM'$.

  • (S1) $M\to_t^*y$. By Fact 0, $M[x:=L]\to_t^*y[x:=L]$. If $y\ne x$, this is $y$, and (S1) applies. If $y=x$, this is $L$, and $L\twoheadrightarrow_sL'$ gives the conclusion by Lemma 2.
  • (S2) $M\to_t^*\lambda z.P$ with $P\twoheadrightarrow_sP'$, the bound variable $z$ being chosen different from $x$ and not free in $L$ or in $L'$. Then $M[x:=L]\to_t^*\lambda z.P[x:=L]$, and $P[x:=L]\twoheadrightarrow_sP'[x:=L']$ by the induction hypothesis: conclude by (S2).
  • (S3) $M\to_t^*P\,Q$ with $P\twoheadrightarrow_sP'$ and $Q\twoheadrightarrow_sQ'$. Then $M[x:=L]\to_t^*P[x:=L]\,Q[x:=L]$, and the induction hypothesis, applied to both subderivations, allows us to conclude by (S3).

Lemma 4. If $M\twoheadrightarrow_sN$ and $N\to_\beta N'$, then $M\twoheadrightarrow_sN'$. By induction on the derivation of $M\twoheadrightarrow_sN$.

  • (S1) $N$ is a variable: it has no redex.
  • (S2) $N=\lambda x.P'$ and the step takes place in $P'$: $P'\to_\beta P''$. By the induction hypothesis, $P\twoheadrightarrow_sP''$, and (S2) gives $M\twoheadrightarrow_s\lambda x.P''$.
  • (S3) $N=P'\,Q'$. If the step takes place in $P'$ or in $Q'$, apply the induction hypothesis to the corresponding subderivation, then (S3). Otherwise the root is contracted: $P'=\lambda y.R'$ and $N'=R'[y:=Q']$. The derivation of $P\twoheadrightarrow_s\lambda y.R'$ necessarily ends with (S2): $P\to_t^*\lambda y.R$ with $R\twoheadrightarrow_sR'$. By Fact 0, $M\to_t^*P\,Q\to_t^*(\lambda y.R)\,Q\to_tR[y:=Q]$. Lemma 3 gives $R[y:=Q]\twoheadrightarrow_sR'[y:=Q']$, and Lemma 2 gives $M\twoheadrightarrow_sN'$.

Proof of the theorem. By induction on the number of steps of $M\twoheadrightarrow_\beta N$: Lemma 1 for zero steps, Lemma 4 to add a step.

Back to normal order. If $N$ is normal, the sequence described by a derivation of $M\twoheadrightarrow_sN$ is the normal-order reduction. This is shown by induction on the derivation, showing also that, if $N$ is not an abstraction, no term of the sequence is one. A term that has a weak head redex is an application, and this redex is the leftmost outermost one; this settles (S1) and the head steps of the other cases. In (S2), the leftmost outermost redex of $\lambda x.P_i$ is that of $P_i$. In (S3), $P'$ is normal and is not an abstraction (otherwise $P'\,Q'$ would be a redex). By the induction hypothesis, no term $P_i$ of the sequence of $P$ is an abstraction; the root of $P_i\,Q$ is therefore not a redex, and its leftmost outermost redex is that of $P_i$. Once $P'$ is reached, it lies in $Q$. We thus recover the previous theorem.

Programming in the λ-calculus

How can one compute without numbers? Church’s idea is to represent the number 3 by the action “do three times”. The number 3 becomes the function that takes an operation and applies it three times in a row. With this idea, addition, multiplication, “true or false” tests and even loops are written as functions.

The λ-calculus has no data structures: everything must be coded by functions. For computability, it suffices to code the integers. Church’s idea is to see an integer as an iterator: the integer $n$ is “apply $n$ times”.

The Church numeral $\mathbf n$ is the term $\lambda f.\lambda x.\underbrace{f(f(\cdots(f}_{n}\,x)\cdots))$; in particular $\mathbf 0=\lambda f.\lambda x.x$. A partial function $f:\N^k\to\N$ is λ-definable if there is a term $F$ such that, for all $n_1,\dots,n_k$:

  • if $f(n_1,\dots,n_k)=m$, then $F\,\mathbf n_1\cdots\mathbf n_k\twoheadrightarrow_\beta\mathbf m$;
  • if $f(n_1,\dots,n_k)\ind$, then $F\,\mathbf n_1\cdots\mathbf n_k$ has no normal form.

In plain words. The number $n$ is represented by the function that applies an operation $n$ times. A function on numbers is “λ-definable” if there is an expression of the λ-calculus that, applied to the representation of $x$, computes to the representation of $f(x)$.

A number stops being a quantity and becomes an action: “three” is “do three times”. This is in fact how young children learn to count, by repeating a gesture. With this idea, a “repeat $n$ times” loop is nothing other than the number $n$ itself.

It is proved in the chapter Equivalence that the λ-definable functions are exactly the recursive functions. This result also shows that the choice of Church numerals is not limiting: any other computable coding of the integers (where one passes from one coding to the other by a term) gives the same class. A non-computable coding, on the other hand, could “hide” information in the representation of numbers.

The substitution $M[0:=N]$ requires shifting the free indices of $N$ when passing under a λ, and decrementing those of $M$ greater than the substituted variable:

def shift(t, d, c=0):                   # adds d to the free indices (>= c)
    if t[0] == "V": return ("V", t[1] + d) if t[1] >= c else t
    if t[0] == "L": return ("L", shift(t[1], d, c + 1))
    return ("A", shift(t[1], d, c), shift(t[2], d, c))

def substitute(t, j, s):                # t[j := s], then the binder is removed
    if t[0] == "V":
        if t[1] == j: return shift(s, j)
        return ("V", t[1] - 1) if t[1] > j else t
    if t[0] == "L": return ("L", substitute(t[1], j + 1, s))
    return ("A", substitute(t[1], j, s), substitute(t[2], j, s))

def normal_step(t):                     # one leftmost-outermost step, None if normal
    if t[0] == "A":
        if t[1][0] == "L":              # redex at the head: (λ.M) N -> M[0 := N]
            return substitute(t[1][1], 0, t[2])
        f = normal_step(t[1])
        if f is not None: return ("A", f, t[2])
        x = normal_step(t[2])
        return None if x is None else ("A", t[1], x)
    if t[0] == "L":
        b = normal_step(t[1])
        return None if b is None else ("L", b)
    return None

Repeating normal_step until None is obtained carries out the normal strategy, which finds the normal form if it exists (theorem). A normal form $\lambda.\lambda.\,1\,(1\,(\cdots 0))$ is recognized as a Church numeral.

The λ-calculus is the basis of functional programming languages: Lisp (1958), Scheme, ML, OCaml, Haskell. “Anonymous functions” (lambda in Python, fun in OCaml, => in JavaScript) are its direct heritage.

Further reading

  • H. Barendregt, The Lambda Calculus, its Syntax and Semantics, North-Holland, 1984: the reference work, very complete but austere for a first approach.
  • J.-L. Krivine, Lambda-Calculus, Types and Models, Ellis Horwood, 1993 (translation of Lambda-calcul, types et modèles, Masson, 1990): more accessible.

The pure λ-calculus is universal, which is why terms like $\Omega$ do not terminate. By adding types, one obtains languages in which every program terminates, and whose programs are proofs: this is the subject of the chapter Logic and types (simply typed λ-calculus, Gödel’s System T, Curry-Howard correspondence).

Exercises

(a) Give the free variables of $(\lambda x.\,x\,y)(\lambda y.\,y\,z)$. (b) Reduce $(\lambda x.\lambda y.\,x\,y)(\lambda z.z)\,a$ to normal form.

(a) In $\lambda x.\,x\,y$, $x$ is bound and $y$ free; in $\lambda y.\,y\,z$, $y$ is bound and $z$ free. So $\mathrm{FV}=\{y,z\}$: the variable $y$ is free in the first factor and bound in the second; these are two independent uses of the same name.

(b) Application associates to the left: the term is $\big((\lambda x.\lambda y.\,x\,y)(\lambda z.z)\big)\,a$.

$$\to_\beta(\lambda y.\,(\lambda z.z)\,y)\,a\ \to_\beta\ (\lambda z.z)\,a\ \to_\beta\ a .$$

With $\mathsf S=\lambda n.\lambda f.\lambda x.\,f(n\,f\,x)$, reduce $\mathsf S\,\mathbf 1$ and check that $\mathbf 2$ is obtained.

$$\mathsf S\,\mathbf1\to_\beta\lambda f.\lambda x.\,f(\mathbf1\,f\,x)=\lambda f.\lambda x.\,f\big((\lambda g.\lambda y.\,g\,y)\,f\,x\big)\twoheadrightarrow_\beta\lambda f.\lambda x.\,f(f\,x)=\mathbf2 .$$

The last two reductions take place under the lambdas $\lambda f.\lambda x$: β-reduction applies to any subterm. The bound variables of $\mathbf 1$ were renamed $g,y$ for readability, which is allowed ($\alpha$-equivalence).

With $\mathsf{tt}=\lambda x.\lambda y.\,x$ and $\mathsf{ff}=\lambda x.\lambda y.\,y$, propose terms $\mathsf{not}$ and $\mathsf{and}$ and check their truth tables.

A Boolean chooses between its two arguments. Set $\mathsf{not}=\lambda b.\,b\,\mathsf{ff}\,\mathsf{tt}$ and $\mathsf{and}=\lambda a.\lambda b.\,a\,b\,\mathsf{ff}$. Then $\mathsf{not}\,\mathsf{tt}\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{tt}\,\mathsf{ff}\,\mathsf{tt}\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{ff}$ and $\mathsf{not}\,\mathsf{ff}\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{tt}$. For $\mathsf{and}$: $\mathsf{and}\,\mathsf{tt}\,b\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{tt}\,b\,\mathsf{ff}\twoheadrightarrow b$, and $\mathsf{and}\,\mathsf{ff}\,b\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{ff}\,b\,\mathsf{ff}\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{ff}$: this is indeed conjunction. Likewise $\mathsf{or}=\lambda a.\lambda b.\,a\,\mathsf{tt}\,b$.

Let $\Omega=(\lambda x.\,x\,x)(\lambda x.\,x\,x)$ and $\mathsf K=\lambda x.\lambda y.\,x$. (a) Show that $\Omega$ has no normal form. (b) Does the term $\mathsf K\,a\,\Omega$ have a normal form? What happens if the arguments are always reduced first?

(a) $\Omega$ contains only one redex, itself, and $\Omega\to_\beta\Omega$. Every reduction sequence is therefore $\Omega\to\Omega\to\cdots$, infinite, and no reachable term is normal.

(b) Reducing the head redex (normal strategy): $\mathsf K\,a\,\Omega\to_\beta(\lambda y.\,a)\,\Omega\to_\beta a$, a normal form. If the argument $\Omega$ is reduced first (“call-by-value” strategy), we loop forever. Both behaviours are compatible with confluence: it guarantees uniqueness of the normal form, not that every strategy finds it. The normalization theorem says that the normal strategy always finds it when it exists.

Let $A=\lambda x.\lambda y.\,y\,(x\,x\,y)$ and $\Theta=A\,A$. Show that $\Theta\,G\twoheadrightarrow_\beta G(\Theta\,G)$ for every term $G$. In what way is this stronger than the property of $\mathsf Y$?

$$\Theta\,G=(\lambda x.\lambda y.\,y\,(x\,x\,y))\,A\,G\to_\beta(\lambda y.\,y\,(A\,A\,y))\,G\to_\beta G\,(A\,A\,G)=G\,(\Theta\,G).$$

Here $\Theta\,G$ reduces to $G(\Theta G)$. For $\mathsf Y$, we only have $\mathsf Y\,G=_\beta G(\mathsf Y\,G)$: the two terms have a common reduct, but neither reduces to the other (computation). With $\Theta$, recursion unfolds by plain computation, which is convenient for programming.

Show that $\mathsf{exp}=\lambda m.\lambda n.\,n\,m$ computes exponentiation: $\mathsf{exp}\,\mathbf m\,\mathbf n\twoheadrightarrow_\beta\mathbf{m^n}$ for $n\ge1$ (write $M^k\,x$ for $M(M(\cdots(M\,x)))$, with $k$ applications).

Lemma. For all $k,j$ and every variable $x$: $\mathbf m\,(\lambda y.\,x^k\,y)\twoheadrightarrow\lambda y.\,x^{mk}\,y$. Indeed $\mathbf m\,g\twoheadrightarrow\lambda y.\,g^m\,y$; with $g=\lambda y.\,x^k\,y$, each application of $g$ adds $k$ applications of $x$ (by induction on the number of applications of $g$, reducing from the inside), whence $\lambda y.\,x^{mk}\,y$.

Then $\mathsf{exp}\,\mathbf m\,\mathbf n\twoheadrightarrow\mathbf n\,\mathbf m\twoheadrightarrow\lambda x.\,\mathbf m^n\,x$. We show by induction on $j\ge1$ that $\mathbf m^j\,x\twoheadrightarrow\lambda y.\,x^{m^j}\,y$. For $j=1$, this is the definition of $\mathbf m$. If it holds for $j$, then $\mathbf m^{j+1}\,x=\mathbf m\,(\mathbf m^j\,x)\twoheadrightarrow\mathbf m\,(\lambda y.\,x^{m^j}\,y)\twoheadrightarrow\lambda y.\,x^{m\cdot m^j}\,y$ by the lemma. With $j=n$: $\mathsf{exp}\,\mathbf m\,\mathbf n\twoheadrightarrow\lambda x.\lambda y.\,x^{m^n}\,y=\mathbf{m^n}$.

For $n=0$, we get $\mathbf 0\,\mathbf m\twoheadrightarrow\lambda x.\,x$, which is not $\mathbf 1=\lambda f.\lambda x.\,f\,x$ but is equal to it up to η-equivalence; this is why the statement excludes this case.
